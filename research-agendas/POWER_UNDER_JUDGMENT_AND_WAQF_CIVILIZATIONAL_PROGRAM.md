# Power Under Judgment + Waqf Civilizational Research Program

**Status:** active research agenda  
**Created:** 2026-08-09  
**Parent canon:** `canon/ACCOUNTABILITY_BEYOND_POWER.md`, `canon/DIGITAL_WAQF_CHARTER.md`  
**Tracking issue:** #103

## Governing question

> **How did Islamic civilization attempt to make power, prestige, and wealth answer to obligations beyond the ruler's appetite — and which mechanisms actually produced durable public benefit?**

This program begins from a strong intuition but refuses the weak formulation.

### Weak formulation to avoid

> Muslim rulers were uniquely benevolent.

That is too broad, too comparative, and too vulnerable to counterexample.

### Stronger research thesis

> **Islamic civilization developed moral and institutional architectures that could redirect status, wealth, and political legitimacy toward durable public benefit — especially through transcendent accountability, waqf, public justice institutions, and prestige attached to beneficence. The historical question is when these architectures worked, how long they lasted, who benefited, and how they failed.**

The target is not civilizational self-congratulation. It is mechanism recovery.

## I. The core mechanism stack

### 1. Power as trust rather than metaphysical ownership

Qur'anic political ethics repeatedly place authority under moral judgment rather than above it. Relevant anchors include:

- Qur'an 4:58 — trusts and just judgment;
- Qur'an 4:135 — justice even against self and kin;
- Qur'an 5:8 — enemy hatred does not suspend justice;
- Qur'an 16:90 — justice and ihsan;
- Qur'an 42:38 — consultation;
- Qur'an 57:7 and related stewardship language — wealth as something humans hold and deploy under a higher claim.

**Research rule:** do not collapse these scriptural ideals into proof of historical implementation. Test the bridge from seed -> system -> fruit.

### 2. Status competition redirected toward beneficence

A ruler, court patron, merchant, scholar, or wealthy family could convert surplus wealth into a durable endowment that generated recurring public benefit and recurring prestige.

This creates a potentially powerful incentive transformation:

> **If elites will compete for status anyway, make hospitals, fountains, kitchens, schools, libraries, roads, gardens, and care institutions status-bearing objects.**

The research task is to distinguish genuine public service from political display, dynastic wealth preservation, patronage capture, and posthumous reputation engineering. These motives can coexist.

### 3. Waqf as mission-locked capital

The waqf separated a revenue-producing asset from ordinary consumption and dedicated its fruits to a defined beneficiary or public purpose.

The civilizational importance is not only generosity. It is **continuity engineering**:

> founder dies -> asset remains -> revenue continues -> beneficiary persists.

This is one of the main hypotheses to test across centuries, geographies, and sectors.

### 4. Justice as ruler-facing obligation

Investigate institutions and practices in which rulers were expected to hear grievances, sit in public judgment, receive petitions, or submit executive action to legal/moral scrutiny.

High-priority cases:

- Nur al-Din and the `dar al-adl` / Palace of Justice;
- Ayyubid and Mamluk petition systems;
- Ottoman petitioning and imperial complaint channels;
- qadi-ruler relations;
- ulama independence and its limits;
- market regulation and hisba where it constrained elite abuse.

### 5. Beneficence as infrastructure, not episodic charity

The strongest cases are not stories of rulers handing out coins. They are systems that kept working after the emotional moment ended.

Prioritize:

- hospitals and medical education;
- food kitchens;
- water supply and fountains;
- schools and libraries;
- traveler infrastructure;
- orphan and disability support;
- debt relief;
- roads and bridges;
- environmental and natural-resource governance;
- animal access to water and care;
- urban greenery where sourceable.

## II. The first flagship: the Haseki Hürrem Sultan Jerusalem waqf

This should become a canonical case study because it combines female elite patronage, public food infrastructure, long duration, archival depth, urban-rural economic integration, and a surviving institution.

### Initial source-closed facts

- Haseki Hürrem Sultan founded the Jerusalem waqf/imaret complex in 1552.
- The endowed complex included a public kitchen, mosque, pilgrim lodging, and an inn.
- Amy Singer's scholarship documents an operating target of roughly 400–500 regular recipients, with two meals a day; contemporary/endowment records specify large daily bread and soup distributions.
- The institution is still active on its original site in Jerusalem today, although its present legal conditions differ from the original Ottoman waqf structure.
- Archival records allow the institution to be studied through its deed, accounts, personnel, repairs, food supply, and changing administration.

### Public-safe formulation

> **A charitable kitchen endowed by Hürrem Sultan in sixteenth-century Jerusalem fed hundreds of people daily and survived as a charitable institution for nearly five centuries.**

### Wording to avoid until further closure

- “It has operated completely unchanged since 1552.”
- “It fed exactly 500 poor people twice every day for 474 uninterrupted years.”
- “This proves Ottoman rulers were uniquely benevolent.”

### Key sources

- Amy Singer, *Constructing Ottoman Beneficence: An Imperial Soup Kitchen in Jerusalem* (SUNY Press, 2002).
- Princeton Near Eastern Studies book record: https://nes.princeton.edu/publications/constructing-ottoman-beneficence-imperial-soup-kitchen-jerusalem
- Julien Loiseau, *La soif de Jerusalem*, discussion of the Haseki institution and its continued activity: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/791
- Abeer Qatanani, “The Charitable Foundation in the Ottoman Period: Al-Imara al-Amira in Islamicjerusalem and its impact on Social life”: https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/beytulmakdis/article/332466

## III. The environmental/public-works bridge: Suleiman's hydraulic waqf

This is the most important immediate connection to the tree-canopy intuition that triggered this program.

In 1541 Sultan Suleiman formalized a hydraulic waqf in Jerusalem around restored water infrastructure and public fountains. The endowed system funded maintenance and repair rather than treating infrastructure as a one-time monument.

The source record describes:

- an aqueduct restored and tied to endowed revenues;
- nine public fountains within the system;
- water for domestic and ritual needs;
- a fountain outside the city useful to travelers, peasants, Bedouins, and animals;
- a contemporary Jewish visitor describing the waterworks as benefiting Jews, Muslims, and Christians;
- repeated maintenance and repair across the Ottoman centuries.

This is a better historical analogue for modern urban green/public-infrastructure politics than trying to retroactively label medieval rulers “environmentalists.”

> **The stronger claim is institutional: Islamic public-benefit law could turn water security into a funded, durable civic obligation.**

Key source:

- Julien Loiseau, “Le waqf hydraulique de Soliman,” *La soif de Jerusalem*: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/791
- Primary-deed translation/annex: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/116571

## V. Longevity test

Every “centuries-long waqf” claim must be decomposed.

Record separately:

1. founding date;
2. original legal deed survival;
3. endowed asset survival;
4. uninterrupted revenue flow;
5. beneficiary continuity;
6. building continuity;
7. service continuity;
8. mission continuity;
9. legal continuity;
10. modern successor status.

A building still standing is not the same as a continuously operating waqf.

A charitable successor on the same site is not necessarily the same legal institution.

A hospital that later became an asylum is institutional persistence, but not uninterrupted medical-service equivalence.

This decomposition is mandatory.

## VI. Red-team: why waqf should not be romanticized

Timur Kuran's work supplies an important adversarial case.

His central criticisms include:

- founder stipulations could make endowments inflexible;
- administrators sometimes circumvented founder rules;
- changing social needs could outgrow the original mission;
- institutional forms could inhibit resource reallocation;
- governments eventually found many waqf resources easier to confiscate;
- waqf did not automatically generate modern-style participatory civil society.

Key source:

- Timur Kuran, “The Provision of Public Goods under Islamic Law: Origins, Impact, and Limitations of the Waqf System,” *Law & Society Review*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/law-and-society-review/article/abs/provision-of-public-goods-under-islamic-law-origins-impact-and-limitations-of-the-waqf-system/6653A7019DB49C8AE0542E017FDCEF54

The research program should use this critique constructively:

> **The question is not whether classical waqf was perfect. The question is which design features produced extraordinary durability, which produced rigidity, and how a modern institution can preserve the first while removing the second.**

## VII. Comparative control

Never establish Islamic excellence by comparing Islamic ideals with another civilization's failures.

Build symmetric cases across:

- Byzantine charitable institutions;
- medieval and early modern Christian hospitals and monasteries;
- Jewish hekdesh/endowments;
- European charitable trusts;
- Hindu temple endowments;
- Buddhist monastic/public-benefit institutions;
- modern foundations and university endowments;
- state welfare systems.

Measure dimensions rather than award a single civilizational trophy:

- duration;
- inclusivity;
- scale;
- autonomy;
- anti-corruption capacity;
- adaptability;
- beneficiary voice;
- resilience under regime change;
- public-goods output per unit capital;
- capacity to protect the politically weak.

> **What would a twenty-first-century waqf look like if it preserved perpetual public-benefit capital but added adaptive governance, transparent accounting, beneficiary voice, scientific evaluation, anti-capture controls, and founder-independent succession?**

## Canonical working line

> **The deepest story is not that some Muslim rulers were generous. It is that Islamic civilization repeatedly tried to make generosity, justice, and stewardship structural properties of power — then built institutions designed to keep serving after the powerful person was dead.**
