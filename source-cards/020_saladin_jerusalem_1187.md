# Source Card 020: Saladin's Capture of Jerusalem (1187)

Date: 2026-08-02

## Claim

On **2 October 1187**, after a siege begun 20 September, Jerusalem passed from the Crusader kingdom to Saladin **by negotiated capitulation rather than by storm** — and the terms, the conduct of the handover, and the fate of the population differ in kind from what the First Crusade did to the same city on **15 July 1099**, when Crusader forces killed its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants over roughly three days.

The negotiation was not a gift and did not begin in mercy. Saladin **refused the first embassy**, replying — in the words the hostile Latin chronicler puts in his mouth — that he had heard from the *fakih* that "Jerusalem cannot be cleansed, save by Christian blood." Terms were extracted only after Balian of Ibelin, defending a city with almost no knights left, threatened mutual destruction: that the defenders would kill their own women and children, burn everything of value, demolish the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa, kill the roughly 5,000 Muslim prisoners held in the city, and die on the walls. What followed was a transaction concluded under a credible hostage threat, and it should be described as one.

The terms themselves were **a published price list**: ten dinars per man, five per woman, and one or two per child (the Arabic and Latin sources differ on the child rate and on the age brackets), with forty days to pay. Roughly 7,000 of the poor were ransomed collectively for 30,000 dinars drawn from the city treasury. **Those who could not pay were enslaved** — about 15,000 people, of whom, by the count of Saladin's own chancery secretary Imad al-Din al-Isfahani, 7,000 were men and 8,000 were women and children. That same secretary — the friendliest witness available, a man on Saladin's payroll writing to glorify him — also recorded the fate of the female captives in an exultant register, of "well-guarded women" profaned and "virgins dishonoured." Nothing in this card is worth more than that sentence, because the enslavement and the sexual violence are attested by the source with every motive to conceal them.

Against that, the documented remissions are also real and also named: Saladin's brother al-Adil was granted 1,000 captives and "immediately released them all"; the Latin patriarch Heraclius was granted 700 to free; the aged who could not pay were freed by order; the ransomed left in three columns under an escort of 50 of Saladin's cavalry; the native Orthodox and Syriac Christians were allowed to remain and worship as they chose; Jews were permitted to resettle a city from which Crusader rule had excluded them; and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre was left standing, with Saladin reported as saying he had no intention to discourage Christian pilgrimage. On the road, the refugees denied entry at Crusader-held Tripoli were robbed of their possessions — by raiding parties from that Christian city, not by Saladin's army.

This is **medieval conquest judged against medieval baselines, not modern humanitarian law**, and the same commander's conduct varied inside the same campaign: eight weeks earlier, after Hattin, roughly **200 captured Templar and Hospitaller knights were beheaded on Saladin's orders**, "choosing to have them dead rather than in prison"; ordinary captured soldiers were sold into slavery, one reportedly for a pair of sandals; the captive King Guy was spared on the principle that "kings do not kill kings" and the noble barons were held well and ransomed. Rank, not mercy, predicted survival.

The claim this card certifies is therefore **comparative and bounded**: measured against 1099 — where Crusader eyewitnesses themselves boasted of riding through blood in the Temple of Solomon and Fulcher of Chartres wrote that "none of them were left alive; neither women nor children were spared" — the 1187 handover was a categorically different event. Enslavement is a catastrophe; it is not the same catastrophe as extermination, and that distinction is the entire load this card carries.

It does **not** show that no one was harmed in 1187 — roughly fifteen thousand people were sold. It does not show that Saladin was consistently restrained — Hattin is in this card for that reason. It does not show that 1099 was total: the Tower of David garrison was conducted safely to Ascalon under Raymond's protection, some inhabitants were permitted to live if they left, and Karaite Jews in Ascalon bought captives back with cash, so even the comparator is not a cartoon. It does not settle the casualty arithmetic of either siege: Ibn al-Athir's 70,000 for 1099 is assessed by modern scholarship as a significant exaggeration against a plausible figure nearer 40,000, and the 1187 enslavement totals rest on a partisan count. And it is **not a doctrinal claim**: one man's conduct in one siege cannot establish what Islamic law requires, and this card refuses that inference explicitly (see Honest Caveats 1 and 9).

## Source Anchors

### The anonymous Latin eyewitness tradition — a hostile contemporary source that confirms the terms

*De Expugnatione Terrae Sanctae per Saladinum* ("The Capture of the Holy Land by Saladin"), ed. Joseph Stevenson, Rolls Series (London: Longmans, 1875), translated by James Brundage, *The Crusades: A Documentary History* (Milwaukee: Marquette University Press, 1962), pp. 159–63, hosted by the Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook:

> On the first embassy: "Meanwhile, they sent legates to the King of Syria, begging him to temper his anger toward them and accept them as allies, as he had done for others. He refused and is reported to have given this reply: 'I have frequently heard from our wise men, the *fakih*, that Jerusalem cannot be cleansed, save by Christian blood, and I wish to take counsel with them on this point.' Thus, uncertain, they returned." On the settlement: "Saladin had taken counsel and laid down these ransom terms for the inhabitants of Jerusalem: each male, ten years old and over, was to pay ten besants for his ransom; females, five besants; boys, seven years old and under, one. Those who wished would be freed on these terms and could leave securely with their possessions." On the deadline: "On Friday, October 2, this agreement was read out through the streets of Jerusalem, so that everyone might within forty days provide for himself and pay to Saladin the tribute as aforesaid for his freedom." On the penalty — **harsher than the Arabic tradition's**: "The inhabitants of Jerusalem who would not accept these terms, or those who did not have ten besants, were to become booty, to be slain by the army's swords." The register of the whole text is openly hostile: it calls Saladin a tyrant and wicked, and the retrieval found **no** passage praising his conduct. It records no act of clemency, and no threat by Balian.

Source: https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1187saladin.asp

This same text is reprinted, from the same Stevenson edition and Brundage translation, under the title "History of Jerusalem: The Capture of Jerusalem by Saladin" — a **mirror of the same chain, not a second witness**, and counted as such:

Source: https://www.jewishvirtuallibrary.org/the-capture-of-jerusalem-by-saladin

### The event, the terms, the enslavement, and the remissions

Wikipedia, Siege of Jerusalem (1187):

> Surrender: "On Balian's orders, the Crusaders surrendered the city to Saladin's army on October 2," after "At the end of September, Balian rode out with an envoy to meet with the sultan, offering surrender." Terms: "ten dinars for men, five for women and two for children; those who couldn't pay would be enslaved." Collective ransom: "He proposed that 7,000 of them would be freed for a sum of 30,000 dinars, and Saladin agreed." Enslavement: "15,000 of those who could not pay the ransom were sold into slavery in the Ayyubid Sultanate," and "According to Imad ad-Din al-Isfahani, 7,000 of them were men and 8,000 were women and children." Remissions: "Al-Adil immediately released them all" (of 1,000 captives granted him); "Heraclius, upon seeing this, asked Saladin for some slaves to liberate. He was granted 700"; "All the aged people who could not pay the ransom were freed by orders of Saladin." Departure: "On Saladin's orders, the ransomed inhabitants marched away in three columns accompanied by 50 cavalrymen of Saladin's army." Aftermath: "The native Christians were allowed to remain in the city and Jews were permitted to resettle as well," and "The Orthodox Christians and Syriacs were allowed to remain and to worship as they chose." Holy sites: "Saladin ultimately decided not to destroy the church, saying that he had no intention to discourage Christian pilgrimages"; "The large golden Christian cross that had been placed over the Dome of the Rock by the Crusaders was pulled down"; "Christian furnishings were removed from the mosque and it was fitted with oriental carpets"; "Its walls were illuminated with candelabras and text from the Quran." The road out: "The remaining refugees went to the County of Tripoli, which was under Crusader control. They were denied entrance and robbed of their possessions by raiding parties from within the city." The comparison, in the article's own words: "The take-over of the city was relatively peaceful especially in contrast to the Crusader siege of the city in 1099." The Aftermath section carries, under the header "Translation of the account of Saladin's secretary Imad al-Din of the treatment of female captives following the siege of Jerusalem," a passage including "How many well-guarded women [were profaned]" and "virgins dishonoured."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(1187)

### The negotiation, and Balian's side of it

Wikipedia, Balian of Ibelin:

> "As the leader of the defense of the city during the siege of Jerusalem in 1187, he surrendered Jerusalem to Saladin on 2 October 1187." The defensive position: "Balian was recruited to lead the defense of the city, but he found that there were under fourteen, possibly as few as two, other knights there, so he created 60 new knights from the ranks of the burgesses." The threat, in the article's summary form: "Balian then rode out to meet with the sultan, to report to him that the defenders would rather kill each other and destroy the city than see it taken by force." Terms: "it was decided that the city would be handed over peacefully, and that Saladin would free seven thousand men for 30,000 bezants; two women or ten children would be permitted to take the place of one man for the same price." And a refused offer: "Balian and Patriarch Eraclius had offered themselves as hostages for the ransoming of the remaining Frankish citizens, but Saladin had refused." Handover: "Balian handed over the keys to the Tower of David (the citadel) on October 2."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balian_of_Ibelin

A tertiary narrative account carrying the fuller text of Balian's threat, by Syed Muhammad Khan (18 May 2020), World History Encyclopedia:

> Balian to Saladin: "we will demolish the Rock and the Mosque el-Aksa (al-Aqsa), and the other holy places, we will slay the Moslem slaves who are in our hands – there are 5,000 such, – and slaughter every beast and mount we have; and then we will sally out in a body to you and will fight you for our lives." Terms: "10 dinars for men, 5 for women, and 1 for children. 7,000 poor people, were to be freed in return of 30,000 gold dinars from the city's treasury" — note the **child rate of 1**, against the Wikipedia article's 2. Outcome: "15,000 people - 7,000 men and 8,000 women - being enslaved," with the article's own summary sentence "the city was taken bloodlessly, though at the price of 15,000 people...being enslaved," and "his triumph was, however, far less violent than that of the medieval knights of the First Crusade (1095-1099 CE)." Remissions: "Saladin's brother al-Adil, Balian of Ibelin, and many ameers (generals) of the Ayyubid army freed people on their own accord. As for Saladin himself, he announced that all elderly people, who could not afford their freedom were to be set free anyway."

Source: https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1553/saladins-conquest-of-jerusalem-1187-ce/

### The comparator: what happened to the same city in 1099

Wikipedia, Siege of Jerusalem (1099):

> "After Jerusalem was captured on 15 July 1099, thousands of Muslims and Jews were massacred by Crusader soldiers." Crusader eyewitnesses, quoted in the article — *Gesta Francorum*: "[our men] were killing and slaying even to the Temple of Solomon, where the slaughter was so great that our men waded in blood up to their ankles"; Raymond of Aguilers: "In the Temple and porch of Solomon men rode in blood up to their knees and bridle reins"; Fulcher of Chartres: "In this temple 10,000 were killed...None of them were left alive; neither women nor children were spared." On the Jews, via Ibn al-Qalanisi: "The Jews assembled in their synagogue, and the Franks burned it over their heads" — with the article's own corroboration limit preserved: "A contemporary Jewish communication confirms the destruction of the synagogue, though it does not corroborate that any Jews were inside it when it was burned." On numbers: "the figure of 70,000 given by the Muslim historian Ibn al-Athir (writing c. 1200) considered to be a significant exaggeration; 40,000 is plausible," against a stated range of "3,000~70,000." And the honesty that keeps the comparator from becoming a cartoon: the Tower of David garrison "took refuge in the Tower of David, and, petitioning Count Raymond for protection, surrendered the Tower into his hands," after which "These Muslims left with the Fatimid governor for Ascalon"; "some Muslim and Jewish residents were allowed to live, as long as they left Jerusalem"; and "the letter of the Karaite elders of Ascalon from the Cairo Geniza indicates that some prominent Jews held for ransom by the Crusaders were freed when the Karaite Jewish community in Ascalon paid the cash ransom."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siege_of_Jerusalem_(1099)

Wikipedia, Massacre of Jerusalem (1099) — the dedicated article, for duration and scholarly calibration:

> The killing lasted "three days" following the initial breach, and the event is characterized as "especially brutal, even by the standards of ancient and medieval warfare" and as "more of a slaughter than a fight." Fulcher "recorded the death toll in the temple alone reached 10,000," while another formulation is "no one knows their number except God alone." The revisionist calibration is present and preserved: "Some historians assert that the severity of the massacre was exaggerated by later medieval sources." Sparing is also recorded: "Raymond caused the Emir and the others who were with him to be conducted to Ascalon, whole and unhurt," and a Muslim group "were granted their lives in return for surrendering."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_Jerusalem_(1099)

### Who the witnesses actually were — the partisanship labels, and the chain collapse

Wikipedia, Imad ad-Din al-Isfahani — **Saladin's own chancery official**, and the source of this card's enslavement arithmetic:

> "Saladin's vizier, al-Qadi al-Fadil, appointed him chancellor, and he also became al-Fadil's deputy." His works: "He wrote the Kitab al-Barq al-Shami, which is largely lost, save for its third and fifth volumes," and "He also wrote al-Fath al-Qussi fi-l-Fath al-Qudsi, which survives." The dependency that collapses the apparent Arabic-side independence: his work "was abridged by al-Bundari and used heavily by the Muslim historians Ibn al-Athir and Abu Shama in their own chronicles." Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imad_ad-Din_al-Isfahani

Wikipedia, Baha ad-Din ibn Shaddad — **not an eyewitness to this siege**:

> "(6 March 1145 – 8 November 1234) was a 12th-century Arabic jurist, scholar and historian." The date of entry into service: "In 1188, returning from Hajj, ibn Shaddād was summoned by Saladin who had read and been impressed by his writings. His biography of Saladin is "based for the most part on personal observation," and he was "an eyewitness at the Siege of Acre and the Battle of Arsuf" — engagements of 1189–91. **He joined Saladin the year after Jerusalem fell**, and this card therefore never cites him as a witness to 1187.

Wikipedia, Ibn al-Athir:

> "ʿAlī ʿIzz ad-Dīn Ibn al-Athīr al-Jazarī (1160–1233) was a Hadith expert, historian, and biographer of Arab descent who wrote in Arabic," author of "a history of the world, al-Kamil fi at-Tarikh (The Complete History)." ## Evidence Grade

**B** — for the claim as scoped in the frontmatter: the 2 October 1187 negotiated capitulation; the published per-head ransom and its forty-day deadline; the collective ransom of 7,000 poor for 30,000 dinars; the enslavement of roughly 15,000 who could not pay, split 7,000 men / 8,000 women and children by Saladin's own secretary; the named remissions (al-Adil's 1,000, Heraclius's 700, the aged freed by order); the 50-cavalry escort and the three columns; the retention of native Christians and the permission for Jews to resettle; the survival of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre; the Hattin executions eight weeks earlier; and the categorical difference from the three-day killing of 15 July 1099. Ten successful live fetches across four distinct publishers (the Fordham Internet Medieval Sourcebook, Wikipedia across eight articles, the Jewish Virtual Library as a mirror of the Fordham text, and World History Encyclopedia) are mutually consistent on every one of these points, with the variances recorded below.

**Refuter-pass record (2026-08-02).** Three lenses were run in-context before this card was written, and each forced a correction the first draft did not contain.

The **primary-source lens** forced four: (1) Baha al-Din ibn Shaddad entered Saladin's service in **1188** and cannot be cited as an eyewitness to the 1187 siege — the case file names him first among the closure targets, and this card demotes him accordingly; (2) Imad al-Din al-Isfahani must be labelled Saladin's chancery official *wherever* his numbers are used, because he is the sole named source for the 7,000/8,000 split; (3) Ibn al-Athir "used heavily" Imad al-Din's work, so the three "independent Arabic chroniclers" of the case file are closer to **one chain with a shared upstream** than to three witnesses — the single largest downward correction in this card; (4) the two chains **disagree on the penalty for non-payment**: the Arabic tradition says enslavement, the Latin text says those without ten besants "were to become booty, to be slain by the army's swords." The card carries both rather than choosing the flattering one.

The **hostile-historian lens** forced the Hattin executions and the Imad al-Din passage on the female captives into the **Claim itself**, not the caveats; forced Balian's mutual-destruction threat and Saladin's initial refusal into the claim, so the terms are never presented as spontaneously offered; and forced the reputation-construction history into the card. It also correctly noted that Ibn al-Athir's 70,000 for 1099 is rejected by modern assessment, so this card cites no 1099 casualty figure without its scholarly discount attached.

The **category-collapse lens** produced the axes note in `grade_scope` and Honest Caveats 1, 9, and 10: the claim layer stays `outcome` and certifies one commander's conduct in one siege; the doctrinal inference is refused and routed to LB-10; the comparative predicate is never permitted to appear without its comparator; and the reception sub-claim is firewalled out of the grade as a separate culture-layer proposition. That firewall is not a formality — the fetched reception evidence **contradicts the intuitive form of the memory claim**, and the card surfaces that against its own interest (see Honest Caveat 8).

It reports the **corroborating** chain, not the load-bearing one, which is why `closure` remains `reconnaissance`: the axis reports the depth of the chain that carries the weight, and the Arabic chain that carries most of this card's specifics was identified and never audited.
5. **Fetch failures, recorded not hidden** (see the transcript). Two specialist-journal articles directly on point were 403-blocked, including one on the changing reputation of Saladin in the Latin West and one contextualizing the 1099 massacre as mass violence — exactly the literature that would discipline this card's two weakest joints. Under the house fetch-failure rule that alone caps at B.

**Dating basis (carried here because closure remains `reconnaissance`):** 2 October 1187 is uniform across all four publishers, and the Latin text independently fixes it — "On Friday, October 2, this agreement was read out through the streets of Jerusalem." The siege's start of 20 September and the forty-day payment window are single-source-family statements. **No Hijri date is asserted anywhere in this card**: none was returned by any fetch, and calendar conversion is arithmetic, not attestation. For the comparator, 15 July 1099 is likewise uniform. Two numeric variances are recorded rather than resolved: the **child ransom rate** (2 dinars in the Wikipedia article, 1 in World History Encyclopedia, 1 besant for boys seven and under in the Latin text, which also brackets by age where the Arabic-derived accounts do not); and the **penalty for non-payment** (enslavement in the Arabic tradition, death in the Latin). A third figure is recorded as search-only and therefore unused: an additional grant of 500 captives to Balian appeared in a search summary but in no fetched page, and is not deployed.

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> **On 15 July 1099 the Crusaders took Jerusalem by storm and spent three days killing its Muslims and Jews — their own eyewitnesses bragged about riding through blood, and one wrote that neither women nor children were spared. On 2 October 1187 Saladin took the same city back, and it changed hands by a signed ransom instead: a published price, forty days to pay, an armed escort out, the native Christians left in place, the Jews allowed back, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre still standing. Fifteen thousand people who could not pay the price were enslaved, and that is a real horror — so tell me which of those two things you would rather have been in, and then tell me again that religious war can only mean revenge without limit.**

## S-Tier Lines

> Same city, same century, two conquests. In 1099 the population was killed. In 1187 it was ransomed. That is not the difference between good men and bad men — it is the difference between two ideas of what victory permits.

> Saladin did not open with mercy. He refused the first embassy and said Jerusalem could be cleansed only by Christian blood. The terms came after Balian told him the defenders would demolish the Dome of the Rock, kill five thousand Muslim prisoners, and die on the walls. Mercy that has to be negotiated at knifepoint is still worth more than a massacre — and it is a far more interesting story than the postcard.

> The number that keeps this honest is fifteen thousand — the people who could not pay and were sold. It comes from Saladin's own secretary, who also wrote down what was done to the captive women. ## Content Angles

### 1. One-line post

> In 1099 the Crusaders took Jerusalem and killed the city for three days. In 1187 Saladin took it back and sold the fifteen thousand who could not pay their ransom. Both of those are atrocities by our standards. Only one of them is a massacre — and the people living there in 1187 knew exactly which one they had been spared.

### 2. Myth-correction hook (corrects both cartoons at once)

> Two false stories circulate about 1187: "Saladin freed everyone," and — in overcorrection — "the mercy was propaganda." The record breaks both. Fifteen thousand people who could not pay ten dinars were enslaved, by the count of Saladin's own secretary, who also recorded the sexual violation of captive women. And the anonymous Latin chronicler who calls Saladin a tyrant writes down the same ransom terms, which is not what happens when a story is invented by the winner's court. It was a transaction extracted under a hostage threat, and it still left the city's population alive, escorted, and in most cases free — which is precisely what 1099 did not do.

## Honest Caveats

1. **Do not convert one man's conduct into a doctrinal claim.** "Saladin acted with restraint at Jerusalem" is an `outcome`-layer fact about one siege. It is not evidence about what the Qur'an, the hadith, or the fiqh require — that is a different layer with different sources, held by LB-10 and the rules-of-war case, which records that Muslim armies repeatedly violated the doctrine they professed. If one commander's conduct varied inside a single campaign (Jerusalem versus Hattin, eight weeks apart), it cannot certify anything about a religion.
2. **Never state the restraint without its comparator.** "Restraint" here is a *comparative* predicate. Detached from 1099 it becomes a false absolute, because roughly fifteen thousand people were sold into slavery. Every deployment must carry the comparison inside the sentence.
3. **Do not present the terms as spontaneously offered.** Saladin refused the first embassy. The negotiated outcome followed Balian's threat to destroy the Muslim holy sites, kill some 5,000 Muslim prisoners, and fight to the death. Report the sequence in order, or the story is false in its most quotable part.
4. **Imad al-Din is a partisan source and must be labelled every time.** He was Saladin's chancery official — appointed chancellor under the vizier al-Qadi al-Fadil. The 7,000/8,000 enslavement split is *his* count. So is the passage on the female captives. Use him, name him, and note that Ibn al-Athir used his work heavily, so agreement between them is not independent corroboration.
5. **Do not cite Baha al-Din as a witness to this siege.** He was summoned into Saladin's service in **1188**, the year after Jerusalem fell, and his eyewitness engagements are Acre and Arsuf. He remains a live closure target for Hattin and the Third Crusade, not for 2 October 1187.
6. **Do not use casualty figures from either siege without their discount.** Ibn al-Athir's 70,000 for 1099 is assessed as a significant exaggeration against a plausible ~40,000, within a stated range of 3,000–70,000; some historians hold the severity was exaggerated by later medieval sources. The 1187 enslavement totals rest on a partisan count. Medieval chroniclers inflate; say the range, or say "thousands."
7. **Do not cartoon 1099 either.** The Tower of David garrison was conducted safely to Ascalon under Raymond's protection; some inhabitants were permitted to live if they left; Karaite Jews of Ascalon bought captives back with cash. The synagogue-burning report from Ibn al-Qalanisi is corroborated as to the building's destruction by a contemporary Jewish communication that does **not** confirm anyone was inside. The 1187 contrast is strong enough without exaggerating the thing it is contrasted with — and exaggerating it is how the contrast gets destroyed.
8. **The memory half of the claim runs the opposite way from the intuition — surface this unprompted.** The fetched reception evidence states that before Kaiser Wilhelm's 1898 Jerusalem visit, "Saladin's Western reputation for chivalry was not reflected in the Muslim world," that "he had been largely forgotten and eclipsed by more successful figures such as Baybars of Egypt," that the visit and anti-imperialist sentiment "led to the reinvention of his reputation by nationalist Arabs," and that modern Arab commemoration is "often based on the image created of him in the 19th-century west." So the durable memory of 1187-as-restraint was carried **first and chiefly in Latin Europe** — Lessing's 1779 *Nathan the Wise* is the fetched exemplar — and re-imported later. Deploying "this became a great Islamic civilizational memory of mercy" as a bare statement is not supported by this card's sources and is the fastest way to lose the argument to anyone who has read the historiography. The honest and stronger line: the conduct is attested by both sides' contemporaries; the *legend* has a documented and largely European construction history; those are two different claims.
9. **Do not let this card carry a generalization it cannot hold.** It is one siege. CMOS-0014 declines to render a blanket verdict on conquest conduct, and this card does not overturn that; it supplies a single dated instance and inherits the refusal.
10. **Do not romanticize the departure.** The ransomed left under escort, but the refugees who reached Crusader-held Tripoli were denied entry and robbed — by raiding parties from that Christian city. That detail belongs in the story because it complicates *both* sides' hero narratives, which is exactly why it is worth telling.
11. **Named scholarly counter-positions exist and were not read.** A revisionist biographical tradition (Andrew Ehrenkreutz, 1972) presses the case that Saladin's reputation is substantially a propaganda achievement, and later scholarship (Lyons & Jackson; Eddé; Phillips) is held to have rebalanced it. These were **identified via search and not read directly**, no page for them is cited as an anchor, and nothing in this card quotes them. Two specialist journal articles bearing directly on both weak joints were fetch-blocked. Treat the scholarly debate as live and unadjudicated by this card.

## Unsafe Wording to Avoid

- "Saladin freed everyone." (About fifteen thousand people who could not pay were enslaved.)
- "The city was taken bloodlessly." (A tertiary source uses this phrase; it is misleading and this card does not repeat it as house wording.)
- "Islam always treated the conquered mercifully." (False, and a category error twice over — one siege is not a doctrine, and one commander is not a civilization.)
- "Muslim conquest was never brutal" / "Saladin never harmed anyone."
- "Saladin was merciful" — stated bare, with no comparator and no ransom.
- "This proves Islam is a religion of mercy." (One commander's conduct in one siege proves nothing about a religion; route doctrinal questions to LB-10.)
- "The first ever humane conquest" or any priority claim of that shape.
- "Saladin invented chivalry" / "the Crusaders learned chivalry from Saladin."
- "Modern human rights in medieval form."
- "Baha al-Din, an eyewitness to the siege, records..." (He joined Saladin in 1188.)
- "Seventy thousand died in 1099" stated as fact.
- "Saladin's mercy is remembered across the Muslim world ever since" (the reception history documented here says otherwise for the pre-modern period).

## Safe Wording

> On 15 July 1099 the First Crusade took Jerusalem by storm and spent roughly three days killing its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants — Crusader eyewitnesses themselves boasted of riding through blood in the Temple of Solomon, and Fulcher of Chartres wrote that "none of them were left alive; neither women nor children were spared." Eighty-eight years later, on 2 October 1187, Saladin took the same city back, and it changed hands by negotiated capitulation instead. That was not saintliness, and it was not free. Saladin refused the first embassy, reportedly saying Jerusalem could be cleansed only by Christian blood; terms came after Balian of Ibelin threatened to demolish the Dome of the Rock and al-Aqsa, kill the city's roughly 5,000 Muslim prisoners, and die on the walls. The terms were a price list — ten dinars a man, five a woman, one or two a child, forty days to pay — and those who could not pay were enslaved: about fifteen thousand people, of whom, by the count of Saladin's own secretary, seven thousand were men and eight thousand were women and children. That same secretary recorded the sexual violation of captive women in celebratory terms. Eight weeks earlier, the same commander had roughly two hundred captured Templars and Hospitallers beheaded after Hattin. Hold all of that, and the comparison still stands — because it is a comparison of categories, not of virtue. In 1099 the population of Jerusalem was killed. In 1187 it was ransomed, escorted out in three columns under armed guard, its native Orthodox and Syriac Christians left in place, its Jews permitted to resettle a city Crusader rule had barred them from, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre left standing, with Saladin reported as saying he had no wish to discourage Christian pilgrimage. The strongest evidence that this is history rather than court flattery is that the anonymous Latin chronicler who calls Saladin a tyrant records the same ransom terms. Enslavement is a catastrophe. It is not the same catastrophe as extermination, and that difference — not any claim about one man's sanctity or one religion's superiority — is the whole of what this record shows.

## Counterattack Simulation

### Counter: "The ransom was extortion, and he sold fifteen thousand human beings. Call that what it is."

Response:

> It is extortion, and it is slavery, and this card says so in its opening paragraphs rather than its footnotes: about fifteen thousand people who could not produce ten dinars were sold, and by the count of Saladin's own chancery secretary, eight thousand of them were women and children. That same secretary wrote down what was done to the captive women in an exulting register. I will not soften any of it, and I am not asking you to admire it. What I am asking is what proposition you think you have refuted. If the proposition were "Saladin was a humanitarian," you would have destroyed it. The proposition is that a city changed hands in 1187 by a published price with a forty-day grace period and an armed escort out, and in 1099 the same city changed hands by three days of killing in which, per a Crusader eyewitness, neither women nor children were spared. Enslavement and extermination are both catastrophes; they are not the same catastrophe, and every person alive in Jerusalem in October 1187 understood the difference in the most personal possible terms. A moral vocabulary that cannot distinguish between them is not a stricter standard — it is a blunter one, and it makes the 1099 dead harder to see, not easier.

### Counter: "The mercy story is Saladin's own propaganda, amplified by seven centuries of European romance."

Response:

> Two-thirds right, and the honest two-thirds is in this card. The Arabic witnesses are compromised: Imad al-Din was Saladin's chancellor, and Ibn al-Athir used his work heavily, so the "three independent chroniclers" collapse toward one upstream — this card grades itself down for exactly that, and it declines to cite Baha al-Din for this siege at all, because he did not enter Saladin's service until 1188. On the romance: the reception history is documented and I will state it against my own interest — before Kaiser Wilhelm's 1898 visit, Saladin's Western chivalric reputation "was not reflected in the Muslim world," he had been "largely forgotten and eclipsed" by Baybars, and his modern Arab commemoration is "often based on the image created of him in the 19th-century west." The legend really does have a substantially European construction history. Here is the third that does not survive your argument. The anonymous Latin chronicler of *De Expugnatione*, contemporary and openly hostile — he calls Saladin a tyrant and a wicked man, and the fetch found not one line praising him — writes down the same ransom scale: ten besants a man, five a woman, one for a small boy, published in the streets on Friday, 2 October, with forty days to pay. Propaganda explains what a court says about itself. It does not explain why the enemy's chronicler confirms the terms while calling you a tyrant. And note that he gives a *harsher* version of the penalty than the Muslim sources do — death rather than enslavement for those who could not pay. That is the signature of an independent hostile witness, not a laundered narrative.

### Counter: "Eight weeks earlier he beheaded two hundred prisoners at Hattin. Your merciful sultan is a myth."

Response:

> He did, and it is in this card's Claim, not buried in the caveats: roughly two hundred captured Templars and Hospitallers were executed on his orders, "choosing to have them dead rather than in prison"; ordinary captured soldiers were sold, one reportedly for a pair of sandals; and by one report he killed Raynald of Châtillon himself. Meanwhile King Guy was spared on the principle that kings do not kill kings, and the barons were held well and ransomed. Rank predicted survival, not mercy. So the myth you are attacking — the flawless chivalric Saladin — is a myth, and the person who most needs to hear that is whoever is deploying him as a cartoon hero. But look at what your point actually establishes: that the same commander in the same campaign behaved very differently depending on the situation, which is an argument that conduct in war is a *choice* rather than a fixed property of a man or a creed. That is this card's thesis, not its refutation. The question was never "was Saladin good." It was "what did power do at Jerusalem in October 1187 when revenge for 1099 was available, expected, and cheap" — and the answer is a price list and an escort, not a three-day sack.

### Counter: "So you are saying Islam mandates humane warfare. One anecdote does not make a doctrine."

Response:

> Correct, and this card refuses that inference before you make it — it is filed on the `outcome` layer precisely so it cannot be read as a claim about scripture or law. One commander's conduct in one siege cannot establish what any religion requires; indeed the same commander's conduct eight weeks earlier at Hattin would embarrass anyone trying to run that argument. The doctrinal question lives on a different layer with different sources, and the archive's entry there carries both halves honestly: an unusually explicit and religiously binding code of restraint for its era, and armies that repeatedly violated it. A companion card in this archive likewise declines to render any blanket verdict on the conduct of the conquests, and this one inherits that refusal rather than quietly overturning it. What a single well-attested case *can* do is defeat a universal negative. "Religious war in that world could only mean revenge without limit" is a universal, and one dated, doubly-attested counterexample is sufficient to break it. That is a smaller job than you are accusing me of doing — and it is the whole job this card was built for.

### Counter: "The 1099 numbers are inflated, so your contrast is inflated too."

Response:

> They are inflated, and this card says so before you do: Ibn al-Athir's 70,000 is assessed by modern scholarship as a significant exaggeration against a plausible figure nearer 40,000, the stated range runs from 3,000, and some historians hold the severity was overstated by later medieval sources. This card cites no 1099 casualty figure without that discount attached, and it also records that 1099 was not total — the Tower of David garrison was escorted safely to Ascalon, some residents were allowed to live if they left, and Karaite Jews in Ascalon ransomed captives back with cash. Now notice that none of that touches the comparison, because the comparison does not run on arithmetic. It runs on the *mode of transfer*. In 1099 the city was taken by storm and there followed roughly three days of killing that Crusader eyewitnesses themselves described in gloating detail — a source class with no motive to exaggerate its own side's slaughter downward or upward for my benefit. In 1187 the city was taken by a negotiated capitulation with published terms that both a partisan and a hostile chronicler record identically. Whether the 1099 dead numbered 3,000 or 40,000, no one in Jerusalem was offered forty days and a price. That is the difference, and it survives every downward revision of the body count.
