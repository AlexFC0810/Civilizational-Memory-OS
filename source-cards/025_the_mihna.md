## Claim

For eighteen years an Islamic polity used the coercive machinery of the state to compel scholars to profess a doctrine about God. In 827 the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun proclaimed the createdness of the Qur'an; in 833, roughly four months before his death, he instituted the test — the *mihna* — that made assent to it a condition of holding office and of being believed in court. The fetched record calls it "a period of religious persecution instituted by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in 833 in which Sunni scholars were punished, imprisoned, or even killed unless they conformed to Mu'tazilite doctrine," and dates the policy at "eighteen years (833–851)," running through al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq and into the reign of al-Mutawakkil, who "abolished it between 848 and 851" and in March 852 "ordered that all prisoners held on account of the Inquisition be released." That is the failure, it is this card's thesis, and no context inside this card makes it smaller.

The mechanism was legal and bureaucratic before it was violent. Al-Ma'mun's letter to his governor instructs him to "gather together the kadis under thy jurisdiction," test them, and tell them that "the Commander of the Faithful will not ask assistance in his govern ment of one whose religion, whose sincerity of faith in God's tinity, and whose [religious] persuasion are not to be trusted"; the governor is then to have his judges "leave ofl accepting as valid the testimony of him who will not confess that it is created and produced." A man who failed the test did not merely lose his post; his word ceased to count in a courtroom. Refusal beyond that ran to "dismissal, arrest or torture."

**The doctrinal instrument of the coercion was the creed of God's unity itself.** When the governor of Baghdad examined the jurist Bishr ibn al-Walid, he "took up a sheet of paper that lay be fore him and read and explained it to Bishr. Then, he said, 'Testify that there is no God but Allah , one and alone , before whom nothing was and after whom nothing shall be and like to whom is nothing of his creation , in any sense whatsoever or in any wise whatsoever'." Bishr's recorded answer — "I testify that and scourge those who do not testify it" — is the whole failure in one sentence: the examined man affirms the oath and volunteers the whip for whoever will not. And when Ahmad ibn Hanbal was bound for flogging, he answered in the same register, complaining that the punishment "was unlawful according to the dec laration of the Prophet, who had said that the blood and possessions of any man who confessed that there was no God but Allah, and that he was God's Messenger, were - inviolable." Both the inquisitor and his victim reached for the same vocabulary. That is what a vocabulary that does not constrain looks like.

**The coercion was narrower in design than the word "inquisition" suggests, and then it did not stay narrow.** Al-Ma'mun's instituting letter "stipulated that the test be administered to qadis and traditionists (muhaddithin)"; the fetched survey is explicit that "the scope of the Mihna was not extended to examining the beliefs of the commoners in the manner of the European Inquisitions." Intensity was uneven: for most of al-Mu'tasim's reign "the mihnah had generally been only lightly enforced," and he "did not pursue the Mihna as anything more than a courtroom formality." Then al-Wathiq "ordered that the severity of the mihnah be increased." And in 845 it reached past officials entirely: the mihna "was even extended to Muslim prisoners held by the Byzantines, when Ibn Abi Du'ad sent an agent to test their opinion on Qur'anic createdness and ransomed only those who supported it, leaving those who did not in Byzantine custody." A state that will leave its own captured subjects in a foreign prison over an answer about God's speech has not run a narrow administrative screening.

**Almost everyone complied, and the tradition's own law made complying licit.** The first seven leading men summoned to al-Ma'mun "all yielded assent under the pressure which al-Ma'mun used with them." In the Baghdad round roughly thirty named jurists and traditionists were called, and on the letter's reading "all of those mentioned" — the OCR carries Patton's marginal heading through the sentence — "in it recanted , with the exception of Ahmed ibn Hanbal, Sajjada, al-Kawariri and Mohammed ibn Nuh al-Madrub." Of those four, one recanted the next day and another the day after, leaving two. Britannica states the general pattern without hedging: "Most acquiesced , utilizing the principle of taqiya (concealment of one's beliefs under duress) to avoid imprisonment." The dispensation is Qur'anic — Patton records that the recanters justified themselves by the verse rendered in his numbering as "Koran, 16. 108": in Abdel Haleem's translation of what is now numbered 16:106, "With the exception of those who are forced to say they do not believe, although their hearts remain firm in faith." Ibn Hanbal himself "always excused" the others on that ground. Patton's own reading is harder than theirs: "but the real cause of their doing as they did was" fear "of execution if they had not done so. Yahya ibn Macin" — the OCR drops a syllable — "ith weeping used to confess that this was the case." The archive should hold both: the tradition supplied a lawful exit from coerced profession, and the men who took it knew what they were doing.

**The violence was real, small in number, and concealed.** Ibn Hanbal was "imprisoned in Baghdad" and under al-Mu'tasim "flogged to unconsciousness"; Patton's account has the lictors "one hundred and fifty in" "number it is said , advanced in turn and each struck him two strokes and then went aside." The state knew it would be condemned: "the scourging took place in the court-yard of the palace unknown to the mass of the people," and when the crowd suspected, al-Mu'tasim produced Ibn Hanbal's uncle and "had this man falsely intimate to them that he had not harmed his nephew in the least." Four men are named in the fetched record as dying under the policy: Muhammad ibn Nuh, who died in custody in transit at 'Anat; Nu'aim ibn Hammad, brought from Egypt under al-Wathiq and "thrown into prison where he died"; Yusuf al-Buwaiti, al-Shafi'i's designated successor, "imprisoned for his refusal to acknowledge that the Koran was created , and died in prison 232 A. H."; and Ahmad ibn Nasr al-Khuza'i, who had also plotted a rising, interrogated on the createdness question and then killed by al-Wathiq in person, who "took al-Samsamah, a famous sword of the pre-Islamic period, and personally joined in the execution" — after which "the trunk and head remained exposed to public view for six years, until the Khalif al-Mutawakkil ordered them to be taken down."

**The reversal did not renounce the power. It re-pointed it.** Al-Mutawakkil "was not a partisan of the traditionalist party"; he "gradually abandoned Mu'tazilism and suspended the mihnah" while purging his predecessors' men, seizing Ibn Abi Du'ad's estates and imprisoning his sons. Turner's abstract states the specialist case directly: the usual answer "assumes that he was acknowledging the inevitable victory of the ulamā," and instead "Al-Mutawakkil ended the Miḥna as one part of his broader effort to establish his position as sovereign and independent of the individuals and structures that had carried over from al-Wāthiq's reign," eliminating the "kingmakers" who had enthroned him. Britannica records what replaced the policy: the inquisition ran "until about 848, when al- Mutawakkil (reigned 847–861) made the profession of the Muʿtazilite view of a created Qurʾān punishable by death." The direction of the lash reversed — "In the years thereafter in the Abbasid state, it was the minority of Muslims who believed in Quranic createdness who were on the receiving end of the sword or lash" — and the same caliph, in the same years, ordered Christians and Jews "to wear a yellow (honey-colored) hood and belt," destroyed "the shrine at Kerbala," and had a man claiming prophecy "imprisoned, and beaten to death on 18 June 850." Nothing in that sequence is a state discovering that belief may not be compelled.

**And the pattern recurred, with the other doctrine winning.** Between 1017 and 1029 the caliph al-Qadir "condemned Mu'tazilite as well as Shi'a doctrines, prohibited the teaching of Mu'tazilite doctrines or theological debate (kalām), and ordered those Hanafi jurists who had shown Mu'tazilite tendencies to do penance, on pain of corporal punishment and exile"; "All speculative theological debate was prohibited"; "Quranic createdness was again singled out as heresy, its supporters branded as infidels 'whose blood may legitimately be shed'"; and the effect was that "the followers of divergent doctrines were denounced as infidels and made licit to be killed as a result." In 1053 the Seljuk vizier al-Kunduri "included the cursing of the Ash'ari school in the khutba of Nishapur," driving al-Qushayri and al-Juwayni to take refuge in the Hijaz. The Mihna is therefore not best read as a single aberration that the system rejected. It is the first and most famous run of a mechanism that ran again.

**Scope-limiter.** This does **not** establish that Islamic scripture licenses compelling belief; the fetched Qur'anic text runs the other way — "There is no compulsion in religion" (2:256), "So can you [Prophet] compel people to believe?" (10:99), "let those who wish to believe in it do so, and let those who wish to reject it do so" (18:29) — which is precisely why the Mihna is legible as a failure rather than as consistency. It does not establish that state coercion of belief was the normal condition of medieval Muslim societies; one fetched page calls the Mihna "one of the few instances of religious persecution among fellow Muslims in Medieval Islam," and this card records that claim in tension with the al-Qadir and al-Kunduri material rather than resolving it. It makes no comparative claim about any other civilization; the Mihna's own encyclopedic entry distinguishes it from "the European Inquisitions" on scope, and this card uses that only to keep the description accurate, never to reduce the verdict. It does not establish any total figure for those tested, punished, or killed — no fetched source gives one, and the fetched record concedes for al-Wathiq's phase that "We have no record of those who were subjected to this examination, beyond the names and accounts of one or two." It does not settle why al-Ma'mun acted; the fetched survey lays out three rival explanations and says only which one "is the position taken up by most modern scholars." And it does not establish that the later Sunni memory of the episode is false — only that it is the winners' memory, and that named modern scholars argue it was reshaped.

### Wikipedia: the policy, its duration, and its declared limits

Wikipedia, "Mihna," retrieved 2026-08-17 as plain text through the MediaWiki extracts API:

> The lead: the Mihna "was a period of religious persecution instituted by the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun in 833 in which Sunni scholars were punished, imprisoned, or even killed unless they conformed to Mu'tazilite doctrine," and "The policy lasted for eighteen years (833–851) as it continued through the reigns of al-Ma'mun's immediate successors, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, and four years of al-Mutawakkil who reversed it in 851." The article's own framing of the significance: "The abolition of Mihna is significant both as the end of the Abbasid Caliph's pretension to decide matters of religious orthodoxy, and as one of the few instances of religious persecution among fellow Muslims in Medieval Islam." Chronology: "In 827, the caliph al-Ma'mun issued the proclamation of the doctrine of Quranic createdness. The proclamation was followed by the institution of the Mihna six years later, approximately four months before his sudden death in 833," and "The Mihna continued under his successors, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, before al-Mutawakkil abolished it between 848 and 851." The scope limit, quoted in full because this card leans on it: al-Ma'mun's motives are discussed as directed at "the members of his government (such as his judges, for the scope of the Mihna was not extended to examining the beliefs of the commoners in the manner of the European Inquisitions)." On al-Mu'tasim's enforcement: he "was afterwards preoccupied with the construction of the new capital at Samarra and with military campaigns, and did not pursue the Mihna as anything more than a courtroom formality (the testimony of a person who answered in the negative was inadmissible in court.)" On Ibn Hanbal: in 833 he "was put to the question, to which he answered that the Qu'ran was uncreated. Al-Mu'tasim removed him from his post, imprisoned him, and had him flogged until he was unconscious. On causation, three explanations are laid out — al-Ma'mun's Mu'tazilism, his pro-Shi'i tendencies, and the assertion of caliphal authority — and only of the third does the article say "This explanation for the Mihna is the position taken up by most modern scholars." Honest absence recorded: this page gives no count of those tested or punished, and names no one who died.

### Wikipedia: the enforcer, the escalation curve, and the historiographic warning

Wikipedia, "Ahmad ibn Abi Du'ad," retrieved 2026-08-17. This is the single densest anchor in the card:

> The apparatus: the mihnah "required officials, judges and scholars to be tested on their beliefs regarding the nature of the Qur'an; individuals who refused to adhere to the position that it had been created were subject to dismissal, arrest or torture," and the chief qadi "also oversaw the appointment of several like-minded qadis to various cities throughout the empire, who similarly prosecuted the mihnah within their respective jurisdictions." The escalation curve, which is why this card refuses to describe eighteen uniform years: "For the majority of al-Mu'tasim's reign, the mihnah had generally been only lightly enforced, although Ibn Abi Du'ad and his allied qadis had escalated their activities in the last year of the caliph's life. With the accession of al-Wathiq, however, the new caliph ordered that the severity of the mihnah be increased, giving advocates of the inquisition approval to proceed with vigor," and "in the provinces there was a marked increase of persecutions against individuals who were considered dissenters." The 845 extension, quoted in full: "In 845 the mihnah was even extended to Muslim prisoners held by the Byzantines, when Ibn Abi Du'ad sent an agent to test their opinion on Qur'anic createdness and ransomed only those who supported it, leaving those who did not in Byzantine custody." The execution: al-Khuza'i's "statements during the interrogation enraged al-Wathiq, and despite Ibn Abi Du'ad's reluctance to have him killed the prisoner was personally executed by the caliph and his soldiers," after which "Ahmad was soon turned into a martyr by supporters of orthodoxy." The reversal's character: "Although he was not a partisan of the traditionalist party, he gradually abandoned Mu'tazilism and suspended the mihnah," inside a general purge in which the new caliph "succeeded in killing or removing from power the majority of these men." The historiographic warning, load-bearing in both directions: modern scholars argue Ibn Abi Du'ad's role "may have been overstated by the sources"; Tayeb el-Hibri holds that "the caliphs were re-imagined as reluctant supporters or even outright opponents of Mu'tazilism and the Inquisition, and the blame for these was instead shifted to Ibn Abi Du'ad"; Muhammad Qasim Zaman calls him a "much maligned figure" and reads the same shift as a traditionalist narrative, because "By making the Inquisition the product of a subordinate official rather than of the caliphs themselves, supporters of orthodoxy hoped to prove the moral bankruptcy of the affair and mitigate the culpability of the caliphs." And the inquisitor is not a cartoon in his own sources: he "was simultaneously praised by the sources for his tolerant and humane nature," and "he is frequently shown as intervening to resolve disputes between the caliphs and their opponents in an effort to prevent bloodshed."

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> In 833 an Islamic state began testing its judges and hadith scholars on a question about the nature of God's speech, and made the wrong answer a bar to office and a disqualification of your word in court. The oath they were made to swear was the creed of God's oneness. When Ahmad ibn Hanbal refused, he was flogged in a palace courtyard out of sight of the crowd, and the caliph sent his uncle out to tell the crowd it had not happened. Nearly everyone else complied, and the tradition's own law let them — the Qur'an excuses the man "who is forced to say" he does not believe "while his heart is secure in faith." Then the policy was reversed, and the reversal did not restore anyone's freedom to answer honestly; it made the other answer the punishable one. Two centuries later a caliph issued a written creed, banned theological debate outright, and declared the losing side's blood licit — and that one held. So the question is not whether Muslims resisted a tyrant over belief. They did, at real cost, and their tradition canonized the one who refused rather than the four caliphs who pressed him. The question is why what the resistance won was a change of custodian rather than a rule that no one may be whipped for an answer — and what that says about a civilizational memory, including this archive's, that has been telling the story as a victory.

### 1. One-line post

### 2. Myth-correction hook (both cartoons, in order)

> Two stories about the Mihna are both false. One says medieval Islam had no inquisition — it did: 833 to about 851, four caliphs, a test for judges and hadith scholars, floggings, prisons, and four men named in the record as dying. The other says the Mihna proves Islam is a religion of enforced belief — also false, and a category error: the text the state was quoting says "There is no compulsion in religion," and the reason nearly every examined scholar could comply with a clean conscience is that the same text excuses a man forced to profess while his heart holds firm. Hold both. The uncomfortable verdict in the middle is the true one: a Muslim state did the specific thing its own scripture forbids, its scholars mostly bent, one of them did not, and what the episode finally settled was not that belief may not be compelled — only who got to say what belief was.

### 3. Debate reply (when the accusation lands)

> I will give you more than you came for. Yes: the Abbasid caliphate ran an inquisition. Al-Ma'mun instituted it in 833, four months before he died, and told his governor that a man whose profession could not be trusted would not hold office and that his testimony should stop being accepted in court. Yes: Ahmad ibn Hanbal was imprisoned and flogged unconscious, and the state did it out of public sight and then lied to the crowd about it. Yes: al-Wathiq took part personally in executing Ahmad ibn Nasr al-Khuza'i — interrogated on this question, and also the organizer of a plot to overthrow him — and left his head on display in Baghdad for six years. Yes: in 845 the empire ransomed Muslim prisoners out of Byzantine captivity only if they gave the right answer about the Qur'an. And yes — this is the part that most damages the case I would rather be making — the reversal did not establish that belief may not be compelled. It made the other profession capital, and two centuries later a caliph published a creed, banned theological argument outright, and had the losers declared infidels whose blood was licit. Now here is what you do not get. You do not get "so Islam commands this," because the text the state was standing on says there is no compulsion in religion, asks the Prophet directly whether he would compel people to believe, and grants an explicit exemption to anyone forced to profess against his heart — which is exactly why nearly every examined scholar complied without apostasy, and exactly why we can call this a failure rather than a consistency. And you do not get a general persecution of the population, because the fetched record says the test was aimed at judges and hadith scholars and was not extended to the beliefs of the general population — that is a limit on the description, not a defence, and I am not offering it as one. The true charge is narrower and heavier than yours: a civilization whose scripture forbids compelling belief built the machinery to compel it, ran it for eighteen years, abandoned it for reasons its own historians still dispute, and never converted the abandonment into a rule.

## Honest Caveats

1. **Do not lead with, or lean on, "the European Inquisition was worse."** The fetched contrast on scope is real and is in the anchors, and its only legitimate job is to keep the description accurate. If it does exculpatory work anywhere in a deployment, the deployment has failed regardless of its accuracy — this is the exact move this repo exists to detect in others.
2. **Do not offer the resistance as mitigation.** Ahmad ibn Hanbal's refusal is real, costly, and in this card at full strength. State it as what it was: a refusal that lost the round, was excused-away by nearly everyone else on lawful Qur'anic grounds, and did not produce a rule against compelling belief. That is both more damning and more true than pretending the tradition was inert.
3. **Do not manufacture a redemption arc, in either of its two available shapes.** The first shape says the resister's canonization proves the system self-corrected; the fetched record answers that the same tradition's authorities held the opposite profession to be capital, and that a caliph re-imposed a creed by decree within two centuries. The second shape says al-Mutawakkil restored freedom of conscience; the fetched record answers that he made the other view punishable by death, imposed distinguishing dress on Christians and Jews, destroyed the Karbala shrine, and had a man beaten to death for claiming prophecy. If you find yourself writing either arc, stop.
4. **Do not assert a count.** No fetched source gives a total for those tested, punished, or killed across eighteen years. What can be deployed is the named roll-call from a single 1897 source, the four named deaths, and Britannica's unquantified "Most acquiesced." Anything else is invention.
5. **Do not present Ahmad ibn Nasr al-Khuza'i as a man executed purely for an opinion.** The same page that records his interrogation on the createdness question records that he had organized a plot to overthrow the caliph. Both facts belong in the same sentence, always.
6. **Do not blame the chief qadi and spare the caliphs — and know why that temptation exists.** El-Hibri and Zaman are in this card's anchors arguing that the shift of blame from the caliphs onto Ibn Abi Du'ad was a traditionalist narrative strategy designed "to prove the moral bankruptcy of the affair and mitigate the culpability of the caliphs." Repeating that shift would be repeating the winners' framing. Note also that the same sources praise the inquisitor for humanity and record his reluctance to have al-Khuza'i killed; he is not available as a monster.
7. **Do not describe eighteen uniform years of terror.** The fetched record says the mihna was "only lightly enforced" for most of al-Mu'tasim's reign and was for a stretch no more than "a courtroom formality," then escalated sharply under al-Wathiq. Overstating the intensity would forfeit the card's credibility on the parts that are damning, which are the design, the 845 ransom test, and the settlement.
8. **Do not claim the Qur'anic verses settle the fiqh.** 2:256, 10:99 and 18:29 are cited here to establish the standard the failure is measured against; the classical exegetical and juristic literature on the scope of 2:256 is contested and was not read this session. And 16:106 is cited as what the recanters actually invoked, which is a historical fact about their reasoning, not a ruling by this card on dissimulation.
9. **Do not deploy "one of the few instances of religious persecution among fellow Muslims" as a defence.** It is a fetched sentence and it is in the anchors, but three other fetched pages document later state impositions of creed. The tension is live; say so, or say nothing about frequency.
10. **Do not treat Patton as current scholarship.** He is public-domain, works from the Arabic chronicles, and supplies the roll-call nothing else fetched this session supplies — and he is 129 years old, writes in a Victorian idiom about "liberals" and "free thinking," and reached this card through imperfect OCR. His single-sourced claims are flagged as such throughout, and his own moral verdicts are quoted as evidence about the historiography, not about the ninth century.
11. **Do not claim this card settles why the Mihna began or why it ended.** Three rival explanations for its beginning are on the fetched record with one named as the majority view; one specialist argument for its ending is on the record in the author's own abstract, against a field consensus the same abstract describes. The article body and the empirical studies are among this card's fetch failures. Grade both C and word them as positions, not verdicts.
12. **Do not read this card as a retraction of CMOS-0011.** It is not one. CMOS-0011's Layer 1 and Layer 2 are textual and stand untouched; its Layer 3 is already graded `probable` and already names the Mihna as its breaker. What this card adds is that the breaker cuts deeper than that card's own wording admits — in particular that the creed was the coercion's instrument and that the settlement relocated rather than limited the power. Whether CMOS-0011 should be amended is the Archivist's call at frontier tier, not this card's.

## Unsafe Wording to Avoid

- "Islam has no history of religious coercion" — false, and the single most damaging sentence a defender can say here, because the Mihna is one search away.
- "The Mihna proves Islam is a religion of enforced belief" — the mirror-image overclaim and a layer collapse; the text the state was standing on forbids compulsion and exempts the coerced.
- "The Mihna was Islam's Inquisition" as a bare equivalence — the fetched record distinguishes them on scope, and the equivalence is not needed for the indictment.
- "Ahmad ibn Hanbal defeated the caliphate" — he was flogged, banished, and outlived three of the four caliphs; what defeated the policy was, on the specialist reading, a fourth caliph consolidating his own power.
- "The Mihna ended and Muslims were free to believe as they chose" — false; the reversal made the opposite profession capital.
- "The ulama won a principle of freedom of conscience" — they won custody of orthodoxy; al-Qadir's creed is the proof of what that was worth.
- "Only the caliphs were to blame" — the enforcement ran through a chief qadi and his appointed judges, and the blame-shift onto that chief qadi is itself argued by named scholars to be a traditionalist narrative move.
- "Thousands were tested" or any numeric claim about the total tested, punished, or killed.
- "Ahmad ibn Nasr was executed for his beliefs" without the coup plot in the same sentence.
- "The European Inquisition was worse, so this was mild" — banned as an opening, a defence, or a pivot.
- "Standards were different then" — the standard invoked here is the tradition's own text.
- "Al-Mutawakkil restored orthodoxy and justice" — he ended one coercion and expanded others in the same years.

## Safe Wording

> From 833 the Abbasid caliphate ran a doctrinal test — the mihna — that required judges, hadith scholars and officials to profess that the Qur'an was created. The stated penalty began as exclusion from office and the disqualification of your testimony in court, and ran to arrest, imprisonment and flogging; four men are named in the fetched record as having died under it, one of them — Ahmad ibn Nasr al-Khuza'i, who was interrogated on that question and had also organized a plot to overthrow the caliph — killed at an execution al-Wathiq took part in personally, though the sources differ on whether the caliph completed it himself, his remains left on public display for six years. Ahmad ibn Hanbal refused, was imprisoned and flogged unconscious, and the flogging was carried out in a palace courtyard out of the crowd's sight, after which the caliph had his uncle tell the crowd he had not been harmed. That is a state coercing belief, and it is a failure of an Islamic polity measured against a standard its own scripture supplies: "There is no compulsion in religion" (2:256); "So can you compel people to believe?" (10:99); "let those who wish to believe in it do so, and let those who wish to reject it do so" (18:29). Three things make the verdict harder rather than softer. First, the instrument of the coercion was the creed itself — the examined jurist Bishr was made to "Testify that there is no God but Allah, one and alone" as the frame of the test, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, bound for the whip, answered by invoking the Prophet's rule that the blood of a man who professes that same creed is inviolable. Both sides held the same vocabulary; it did not decide the question. Second, almost everyone complied — of roughly thirty men summoned in Baghdad all recanted but four, and two of those recanted within two days — and they could do so lawfully, because the Qur'an excuses one "who is forced to say" he does not believe while "his heart is secure in faith" (16:106), a dispensation Ahmad ibn Hanbal himself used to excuse them. Third, and this is the part that refuses a happy ending: when al-Mutawakkil wound the policy down between 848 and 851 and released the Inquisition's prisoners in March 852, what replaced it was not liberty of conscience. The profession of the created Qur'an was itself made punishable by death; in the years after, it was those who held that view who were "on the receiving end of the sword or lash"; the same caliph ordered distinguishing dress for Christians and Jews, destroyed the Shi'a shrine at Karbala, and had a man beaten to death for claiming prophecy; and between 1017 and 1029 the caliph al-Qadir issued a written creed that prohibited theological debate outright, ordered penance from suspect jurists "on pain of corporal punishment and exile," and had the losing doctrine's holders branded infidels whose blood might legitimately be shed. A specialist reading of the reversal, published in Oriens, argues that al-Mutawakkil ended the mihna "as one part of his broader effort to establish his position as sovereign," against the "kingmakers" who had enthroned him — not because the scholars had won. So the honest verdict is this: the resistance was real and costly, the tradition canonized the resister rather than the caliphs who broke him, and what the episode settled was not that belief may not be compelled — only who held the authority to say what belief was. What it is not is evidence that Islamic scripture licenses compelled belief; that is a different claim on a different layer, and it needs its own evidence, which this is not. What remains, and what this card is for, is the failure: an Islamic state built the machinery to compel belief, four caliphs ran it, and the settlement that ended it left the power to compel intact in other hands.

## Counterattack Simulation

### Counter: "You have just handed every anti-Muslim polemicist a ninth-century inquisition, sourced, and called it scholarship."

Response:

> The Mihna is in Britannica and in every survey of Abbasid history; it is not a secret this archive is leaking. What is being decided is not whether an audience learns it, but whether they learn it from us with the evidence in order, or from someone who will also tell them that Islamic scripture commands enforced belief and that this was the Spanish Inquisition in Arabic — two claims this card refutes and that a defender caught denying the floggings will have no standing to refute. And there is a structural reason this card exists that has nothing to do with polemics. A valence audit of this archive found twenty-three of twenty-three gated theses exculpatory. An institution whose corpus can only tilt one way has no credible verdict when it says something favourable either, because nobody can distinguish its findings from its preferences. This card is aimed at our own load-bearing beam. If we will card the Mihna against CMOS-0011, our other cards mean something.

### Counter: "The Mihna was narrow — judges and hadith scholars, not ordinary Muslims — and it was lightly enforced for most of its run. You are inflating an administrative loyalty test into an inquisition."

Response:

> Both halves of your premise are conceded, and they are in the card's own Claim rather than buried: the fetched record says the test was stipulated for qadis and traditionists, that its scope "was not extended to examining the beliefs of the commoners in the manner of the European Inquisitions," that for most of al-Mu'tasim's reign it was "only lightly enforced," and that it functioned at times as no more than "a courtroom formality." Now here is where the inference fails. A loyalty test does not flog a man to unconsciousness and then lie to a crowd about having done it. A loyalty test does not end with a caliph taking a sword and joining the execution personally, and a head on display for six years. And above all, a loyalty test confined to the judiciary does not, in 845, follow Muslim prisoners into Byzantine captivity and ransom only those who give the correct answer about the nature of the Qur'an — leaving the rest with the enemy. Narrow in design; not narrow in what it was willing to do. The scope point survives as a description. It does not survive as a defence.

### Counter: "This is exactly backwards. The Mihna is the great proof that Islamic civilization rejects caliphal control of doctrine. The caliph lost. Ibn Hanbal won. The ulama's independence dates from that victory."

Response:

> This is the strongest objection on the board, it has real scholarship behind it, and part of it is simply true. The policy was abandoned; the traditionalist school emerged, in the fetched wording, with "its prestige strengthened and its doctrines more influential than before"; and the tradition's memory made the flogged scholar canonical while the enforcing chief qadi became "an object of vilification." Three concessions, unforced. But the objection requires that what was won was a *limit*, and the record will not carry that. First, on the fetched account of the reversal itself, the reversing caliph "was not a partisan of the traditionalist party" and ended the policy inside a general purge of his predecessors' men — and the specialist article on precisely this question argues that the field's usual "the ulama won" reading is wrong and that he was establishing his own sovereignty against the kingmakers. Second, and decisively: the very same reversal made the opposite profession punishable by death, and in the years after it was the createdness party who felt the lash. Third: within two centuries a caliph issued a creed by decree, prohibited theological debate outright, and declared the losing doctrine's holders infidels whose blood was licit — the identical pretension, exercised successfully, in the winners' favour. If the Mihna had established that no ruler may define belief, al-Qadir's creed would have been unthinkable. It was not unthinkable. It became the definition of Sunni orthodoxy.

### Counter: "So your own archive's tawhid card is propaganda. You argued Islam has a built-in check on absolutism, and here is an Islamic state coercing belief with the whip."

Response:

> Not propaganda — under-specified, and this card says so in its own opening rather than waiting to be asked. Here is the precise damage. CMOS-0011 argues that tawhid supplies a standing vocabulary against human absolutism. The Mihna shows that vocabulary in the hands of the coercer: the test was framed as an oath that there is no god but God, one and alone, and the caliph's letter made "sincerity of faith in God's unity" the qualification for office. It also shows it in the hands of the coerced: Ahmad ibn Hanbal, bound to the post, argued that the blood of a man who professes that creed is inviolable. A vocabulary both sides can hold is not yet a constraint — it is a language in which the fight is conducted. That is a real narrowing of CMOS-0011's Layer 3, and this card registers it. What it does not do is vindicate the opposite claim. The tradition's founding text says there is no compulsion in religion and asks the Prophet directly whether he would compel people to believe. That is why nearly every examined scholar could comply without apostasy, under an explicit exemption for the coerced — and it is why the Mihna reads as a failure rather than as a system operating normally. An archive that could not say this about its own card would not be worth reading on anything else.

### Counter: "Every pre-modern state policed belief. Singling out the caliphate is bigotry with citations."

Response:

> The premise is true and the inference is a non-sequitur, and it is worth being precise about why. This card makes no comparative ranking; it does not claim the Abbasids were worse than any contemporary, and it explicitly refuses the claim that coerced orthodoxy was a distinctively Islamic vice. What it claims is that *this* polity, which is the one this archive exists to remember accurately, compelled a profession of belief by whip and prison, and that the settlement afterwards changed the punishable belief rather than the practice. That proposition would be exactly as true if every other eleventh-century state had been worse. Now the reversal, because the selective standard runs the other way here: "everyone did it" is invoked almost exclusively by defenders, and almost never by the same people when a rival civilization's record is under discussion — and this repo's stated purpose is detecting precisely that move in others. We cannot run a distortion detector that exempts its own subject. There is also a specific reason the move fails on these facts. A universal-practice defence works against a charge of unique depravity. The charge here is failure against an internal standard: a text that says there is no compulsion in religion, and a state that built the apparatus of compulsion anyway. Nobody else's record has any bearing on whether this civilization met the standard it wrote for itself.

### Anti-fabrication re-verification (2026-08-17)

Thirty-eight quoted strings — every excerpt this card leans on — were re-checked after drafting against a **fresh live retrieval** of each source, not against the copy retrieved during research. The check normalised only whitespace and curly quotation marks, and required exact substring presence. Nothing was quoted that failed verification, and no quotation in this card is fabricated or altered in meaning.

**Correction, entered 2026-08-17 after an adversarial re-audit — the original wording here overstated this card's own audit and is retracted.** This section previously read "all thirty-eight were found present" and "no string was repaired into agreement with the card." Both were too strong. A hostile re-check against a fresh Internet Archive retrieval found that a minority of the Patton strings are **not contiguous in the OCR at all**: printed marginal side-headings sit *inside* them. "with the exception of Ahmed ibn Hanbal" bridges the marginal fragment "Doctors." sitting between "ibn" and "Hanbal"; the side-headings "Recantation of the Doctors." and "Death of Ibn Nuh." exist nowhere as contiguous strings, being three and two interleaved fragments respectively; and at least one space and one marginal asterisk were normalised away. Those strings were therefore verified by **tracing across OCR fragments, not by exact substring match** — a weaker and slower operation than the one originally claimed.

No meaning changed, no source was misrepresented, and the underlying quotations hold. But a card whose evidentiary currency is exact reproduction cannot overstate the rigour of its own verification, and the failure mode is the one this archive keeps paying for: **a check described more confidently than it was performed is the same defect as a check that reports healthy while blind.** The claim is now stated at the strength the evidence supports.

Scope limit of this check, stated so it is not read as more than it is: it proves that each quoted sentence exists in the source as fetched today, from two separate retrievals. It does not audit whether the sources are right — Wikipedia and Britannica are tertiary, Patton is 129 years old, and the Arabic chronicles beneath all of them were not read. That is why closure is `reconnaissance` and the grade is B.
