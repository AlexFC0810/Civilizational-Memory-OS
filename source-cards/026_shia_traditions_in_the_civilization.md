# Source Card 026: The Ismaili Founding of al-Azhar, and Its Erasure

Date: 2026-08-17

## Status

**Deployment status:** reconnaissance-closure draft through the mechanical gate, graded **B**. The ceiling is held down by five fetch failures and by the fact that no primary text was read — no waqf deed, no foundation inscription in situ, no chronicle of al-Maqrizi or al-Mufaddal in its own words, no Fatimid da'wa text. Closure stays `reconnaissance`.

**Why this card exists.** `archive/LOAD_BEARING_INDEX.md` lists blind spot (3) and describes it exactly: *Shia/Zaydi/Ismaili Islam remains entirely uncarded* — "a serious gap, since it is a confessional absence rather than a regional one." Of twenty-five gated cards, none was about Shia Islam, and the index therefore concedes that it "still maps the Sunni core of the story, and says so." This card closes that gap on one claim, and it deliberately does not close it the comfortable way.

**Which half is the thesis, and which half is subordinated — stated up front because the assignment turns on it.** This card carries both halves required of it: the contribution and the intra-Muslim persecution, including Fatimid-side coercion. The **contribution is the premise; the persecution is the thesis.** The reason is not tone-setting, it is structural: "Ismaili Shia Muslims founded al-Azhar" is, on its own, a fact about a building and a date. It becomes load-bearing only in conjunction with what was done to the founders' school afterwards, and it is that conjunction the card gates. A card that closed a confessional gap by reporting only that Shia Muslims also built things would have added a twenty-sixth exculpatory thesis to a corpus whose own audit found that problem, and would have closed nothing. `thesis_valence: failure` is therefore assigned to what the card actually argues.

**Layer derivation.** The pre-allocation would suggest `institution`; the evidence says `outcome`. What is graded here is a historical result — what Muslim rulers, jurists, and educational authorities did to a confessional minority across the Ayyubid, Mamluk, and modern Egyptian states — not the institutional facts of al-Azhar as such. `institution` is also the archive's most saturated layer, but that is a tiebreaker, not the reason; if the evidence had supported an institution-layer thesis this card would have carried one.

**Best use:** as the confessional counterpart to CMOS-0024. It also collides directly with **CMOS-0020**, which grades Saladin's conduct at Jerusalem in 1187 as an achievement. Both cards are true. Deploying either one without the other produces a false picture of the same man, and this card takes CMOS-0020's grade as binding on its own scope while refusing to let that scope expand.

## Claim

**The flagship institution of Sunni Islam was built by Ismaili Shia Muslims, and the civilization that inherited it suppressed the school that built it: banned its congregational prayers, withdrew its students' and teachers' stipends, destroyed the Fatimid-teaching manuscripts held in its library, and stripped the founders' names from its mihrab — and the exclusion of that confession has a documented present tense in Egypt, established on separate modern evidence rather than as an unbroken chain.**

The founding is not in dispute and is not obscure. Al-Azhar was commissioned with the new Fatimid capital in 969, begun on 4 April 970 by the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli, completed in 972, and — in the fetched record's own words — "had al-Azhar built as a base to spread Isma'ili Shi'a Islam." A second, entirely separate publisher, the Metropolitan Museum of Art's *Heilbrunn Timeline*, gives the same reading independently: al-Azhar, "founded along with the city (969–73), which, together with its adjacent institution of higher learning (al-Azhar University), became the spiritual center for Isma'ili Shi'i." Under Fatimid rule "the previously secretive teachings of the Isma'ili madh'hab (school of law) were made available to the general public," classes ran with "separate sessions available to women," the Fatimid vizier Ya'qub ibn Killis — a Baghdad-born Jew who converted to Islam in 967 — "made al-Azhar a key center for instruction in Islamic law in 988," and the mosque, "initially lacking a library," was "endowed by the Fatimid caliph in 1005 with thousands of manuscripts that formed the basis of its collection." Encyclopaedia Iranica, whose Fatimids article is the specialist reference in this file, calls the same period "the golden age of Ismaʿili thought and literature." Today that institution "has long been regarded as the foremost institution in the Islamic world for the study of Sunni theology and sharia."

What happened between those two sentences is the claim. Saladin ended the dynasty in 1171 and, in the fetched wording, "was hostile to the Shi'ite principles of learning propounded at al-Azhar during the Fatimid Caliphate, and under his Ayyubid dynasty the mosque suffered from neglect." The specifics are itemised in the source and they are not neglect: "Congregational prayers were banned by Sadr al-Din ibn Dirbass, appointed qadi by Saladin." "Student funding was withdrawn, organized classes were no longer held at the mosque, and the professors that had prospered under the Fatimids were forced to find other means to earn their living." "The once well stocked library at al-Azhar was neglected, and manuscripts of Fatimid teachings that were held at al-Azhar were destroyed." And, most literally: "Saladin also ordered the removal from the mihrab of the mosque a silver band on which the names of the Fatimid caliphs had been inscribed." The founders' names were taken out of the wall they had built, melted with similar bands from other mosques for a recorded total of 5,000 dirhems. Meanwhile "Educational institutions were established by Sunni rulers as a way of combating what they regarded as the heretical teachings of Shi'a Islam," and the Ayyubids "would build twenty-six colleges in Egypt." The lead summary of the whole millennium states the causation without hedging: "Because it was founded as a Shiite Ismaili institution, Saladin and the Sunni Ayyubid dynasty that he founded shunned al-Azhar, removing its status as a congregational mosque and denying stipends to students and teachers at its school."

The rehabilitation is the part that should be read slowest, because it is where the erasure completes rather than reverses. Congregational prayer resumed under the Mamluk sultan Baibars in 1266, with "the first khutbah since the reign of the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim" delivered on 16 January of that year, on a mosque "which had been neglected for nearly 100 years." The stated precondition is explicit: "Al-Azhar had by now lost its association with the Fatimids and Ismāʿīli doctrines, and with Cairo's rapid expansion, the need for mosque space allowed Baibars to disregard al-Azhar's history and restore the mosque to its former prominence." The institution was restored once it had stopped being theirs. That sentence is the card, and the word "erasure" in its title is the archive's characterisation of that sequence, not a phrase any source uses — a distinction stated here so it is never smuggled.

The present tense is documented by a publisher with no stake in medieval historiography. The US State Department's 2023 report on international religious freedom in Egypt records that Shia Muslims are "approximately 1 percent of the population"; that, per Minority Rights Group International, "there continued to be no Shia congregational halls (husseiniyas) in the country and Shia Muslims remained unable to establish public places of worship"; that "Some Shia stated they were excluded from service in the armed forces and from employment in the security and intelligence services"; that "anti-Shia posts made up more than 79 percent of hate speech posts analyzed by the center" on one platform; and — this is the sentence that closes the arc — that in a review of the al-Azhar religious-school curriculum, "A textbook on branches of Islam other than Sunni considers most Shia Muslims, including Twelvers and Zaydis, as legitimate but rejects Ismailis, Ahmadis, and Baha'is as heretical." The confession that founded the institution is classed as heretical in the institution's own schoolbooks, on the finding of an outside curriculum monitor, in a report of 2023.

**The other half, at full strength, because a card that only prosecutes one side is not an archive.** Fatimid rule was itself coercive, and the tradition being defended here does not need protecting from that. The caliph al-Hakim, who founded the Dar al-'Ilm in 1005 and "provided paper, ink, pens and inkstands free of charge to all those who studied there," also imposed the *ghiyar* dress restrictions on Jews and Christians in 1005, "ordered the destruction of the Holy Sepulchre and its associated buildings" on 18 October 1009, and "ordered or sanctioned the destruction of a number of churches and monasteries (mostly Coptic or Melkite), which was unprecedented." Under his successor al-Zahir "the Druze faced a mass persecution, which included large massacres against the Druze in Antioch, Aleppo, and other cities" — a Shia state massacring a group that had split from it. Ismaili doctrine "was kept secret from those who were not initiated." And the Ismailis never became the majority of anything they ruled: "It's unclear what number or percentage of the population inside the caliphate were actually Isma'ilis, but they always remained a minority," while "In Egypt, the majority of Muslims were Sunni and remained so throughout the Fatimid period." The tidy binary fails in the other direction too: when the Abbasid caliph al-Qadir moved against the Fatimids in 1011, "he assembled a number of Sunni and Twelver Shiite scholars at his court and commanded them to declare in a written document that Hakim and his predecessors lacked genuine descent from Ali and Fatima," a manifesto "read out in Friday mosques throughout the 'Abbasid domains accusing the Fatimids of Jewish ancestry." Twelver scholars signed the document against the Ismaili claim. The line this card draws is not Sunni against Shia; it is power against a minority confession, and Shia rulers were on the persecuting side of it when they held it — though not invariably, and this card does not claim otherwise: the Buyids, a Shia dynasty ruling Baghdad over a Sunni majority, are described in the same fetched record as "tolerant of the Sunni population."

This does **not** establish a doctrinal verdict between Sunni, Twelver, Ismaili, or Zaydi Islam, in any direction: that is a `fiqh`-layer question with its own evidentiary requirements and this card certifies nothing on it. It does not establish that Sunni Islam is inherently sectarian, which the same record refutes — the Buyids, a Shia dynasty holding Baghdad from 945, "were known for supporting the Sunni Abbasid caliphs and being tolerant of the Sunni population, who were the majority in their realm"; Zaydis "historically were quite tolerant towards Shafi'i Sunnism"; Abu Hanifa, the founder of the largest Sunni school, "delivered a fatwā or legal statement in favour of Zayd in his rebellion" and "secretly urged people to join the uprising and delivered funds to Zayd"; al-Azhar itself, under Mahmud Shaltut in 1959, issued a fatwa "which essentially declared that worship according to the Twelver Shia jurisprudence was valid," reissued by Ahmed el-Tayeb in 2016; and the 2004–05 Amman Message, which the index already carries at LB-47, names "Shia Jaʿfari" among the eight recognised schools whose followers' blood is inviolable. It does not establish any figure for the Fatimid palace library, whose only fetched number is an unattributed range "from 120,000 to 2,000,000." It does not establish Saladin's motive: the source itself says the ban on congregational prayer "may have been" Shafi'i doctrine "or mistrust of the former Shi'a institution," and leaves it there. It does not establish that al-Azhar today has a formal policy barring Shia instruction — that rests on one community member's statement as recorded by the State Department, and is reported here as exactly that. And it does not assert a continuous 850-year intention, which no source states and which the record contradicts, since the Mamluk restoration was indifference to the history, not hostility to it.

### The wider pattern, and the epistemic problem it creates

Wikipedia summaries. Anti-Shi'ism:

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> The institution "long regarded as the foremost institution in the Islamic world for the study of Sunni theology and sharia" was built by Ismaili Shia Muslims. ## Safe Wording

> Al-Azhar — today the foremost institution in the Islamic world for the study of Sunni theology and sharia — was founded by an Ismaili Shia dynasty. The point is what came next. Saladin ended the dynasty in 1171, and the record states the consequence without hedging: "Because it was founded as a Shiite Ismaili institution, Saladin and the Sunni Ayyubid dynasty that he founded shunned al-Azhar, removing its status as a congregational mosque and denying stipends to students and teachers at its school." Congregational prayers were banned by the qadi Saladin appointed; the professors "were forced to find other means to earn their living"; "manuscripts of Fatimid teachings that were held at al-Azhar were destroyed"; and Saladin "ordered the removal from the mihrab of the mosque a silver band on which the names of the Fatimid caliphs had been inscribed." When Friday prayer returned under Baibars in 1266, the stated precondition was that al-Azhar had by then "lost its association with the Fatimids and Ismāʿīli doctrines," which allowed the sultan "to disregard al-Azhar's history." And the sequence has a present tense: the US State Department's 2023 religious-freedom report records that Shia Muslims in Egypt "remained unable to establish public places of worship," that there are no husseiniyas in the country, and that an outside review of the al-Azhar school curriculum found a textbook that treats Twelvers and Zaydis as legitimate while it "rejects Ismailis, Ahmadis, and Baha'is as heretical." The Fatimids are not owed a clean version of this, and they do not get one here: al-Hakim imposed differentiation laws on Jews and Christians in 1005 and ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in 1009; his successor's reign brought mass persecution of the Druze; Ismaili doctrine was kept from the uninitiated; and Ismailis "always remained a minority" in their own state, ruling a population that stayed Sunni throughout. Nor is this Sunni against Shia: in 1011 the Abbasid caliph assembled "a number of Sunni and Twelver Shiite scholars" to deny the Fatimids' descent from Ali and Fatima, and the Shia Buyids in Baghdad were tolerant of their Sunni majority and hostile to the Fatimids. I am not going to tell you other civilizations did the same to their minorities, because that is not an answer to this. The honest charge is narrower and heavier: Islamic civilization kept the building and lost the builders, and it is still losing them. What this is not is a verdict on any theological question between Sunni, Twelver, Ismaili, and Zaydi Islam — that is a different claim on a different layer, needing evidence this card does not have and does not pretend to.
