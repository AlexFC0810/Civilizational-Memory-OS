# Source Card 022: Maritime Southeast Asia — Islamization Without an External Conquest

Date: 2026-08-02

## Status

**Deployment status:** reconnaissance-closure draft through the mechanical gate; graded B, pending frontier adjudication (grade-A promotion is not this tier's call — see Evidence Grade for what would justify it).

**Best use:** the missing proof under a claim the archive already deploys. `archive/LOAD_BEARING_INDEX.md` confesses that "Shia/Zaydi/Ismaili and Southeast Asian Islam (incl. Indonesia, the largest Muslim population) are nearly absent from the graded corpus," and that until carded the index "maps the Sunni-Arab/Persian core of the story." Meanwhile CMOS-0014 (`source-cards/014_conquest_and_conversion.md`) already uses Indonesia as its sharpest anvil against "spread by the sword." This card supplies the regional evidence base that anvil was resting on, and does one thing 014 does not: it establishes the correction as well as the case.

**Breaker surfaced unprompted (read this before deploying either card).** CMOS-0014 says of Indonesia: "the land that hosts the largest Muslim population on earth is one no Muslim army ever conquered." As an absolute that phrasing does not survive this card's evidence. No *outside* Muslim empire conquered the archipelago — that half is solidly attested, including by a US Library of Congress country study which states flatly that "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted." But *local* Muslim armies conquered plenty of it once local dynasties had turned Muslim: Demak "conquered the last Javanese Hindu-Buddhist state"; the Sunda kingdom of Pajajaran "was conquered by Muslims in the 16th century"; and in 1520 Aceh's Ali Mughayat Syah "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam." The defensible wording is **"not conquered into Islam from outside,"** not "no Muslim army ever conquered it." This card does not edit 014 — the wording fix is the Chief of Staff's call — but the archive should not ship the absolute while this card sits beside it.

**House style:** *not by external conquest*, never *peaceful*. "Peaceful" is the overclaim that gets a creator destroyed on camera by one Aceh citation; "no Arab or Persian army did this" is the claim that holds. Lead with the mechanism (ships, marriages, courts, teachers), concede the local force in the same breath, and refuse the doctrinal leap out loud — a region that converted without foreign conquest tells you how Islam travelled in the Indian Ocean world, not what Islam commands.

## Claim

Maritime Southeast Asia — the Indonesian archipelago, the Malay peninsula, Brunei, and the southern Philippines — became predominantly Muslim without being conquered by any outside Muslim power. No caliphal, Arab, Persian, Turkish, or Mughal army carried Islam into the region. The channels the sources actually describe are commercial and social: Muslim merchants settling in the trading ports of the Indian Ocean and South China Sea routes; intermarriage with local elites ("the spread of Islam among the ruling class was precipitated as Muslim traders married the local women, with some of the wealthier traders marrying into the elite ruling families"); the conversion of local rulers, after which "their subjects mirrored their conversion"; and Sufi teachers, whom the record treats as "a more probable agent of religious conversion" than merchants alone and who worked through local forms — one of the Javanese *wali* is credited with having "used wayang kulit shadow puppets and gamelan music to convey spiritual teachings." The process was long and uneven, not a switch: Arab Muslim traders are attested in the region "as early as the 8th century," but "it was not until the end of the 13th century that the spread of Islam began," it "accelerated in the 15th century," it took until "the 17th century" for the majority of the Malay peninsula, and the evidence "does not suggest a rolling wave of conversion through adjacent areas; rather, it suggests the process was complicated and slow," moving faster on the coasts than in the interiors. The demographic result is the largest national Muslim population on earth: Indonesia, about 249.8 million Muslims, "which on its own hosts 13% of the world's Muslims."

The absence of an *outside* conquest did **not** mean an absence of force, and this card puts that in the claim rather than a footnote. Once local dynasties became Muslim, their wars became Muslim wars: a Library of Congress country study records in the same breath that "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted" and that "states that had converted to Islam often waged war against those that adhered to the older, Hindu-Buddhist traditions." Demak, a Muslim port-fief that broke from Majapahit, "conquered the Hindu-based resistance in Central Java" and then "conquered the last Javanese Hindu-Buddhist state"; the Hindu-Buddhist Sunda kingdom of Pajajaran "was conquered by Muslims in the 16th century"; and in 1520 Aceh's Ali Mughayat Syah "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam" — the single sharpest fetched instance of coerced Islamization in the archipelago. Muslim polities here also ran coercive economies: the Sulu sultanate's raids captured people "to be sold at slave markets," and "as much as 50% of the population of Sulu in the 1850s were bisaya slaves."

Conversion was also syncretic and incomplete. "Islam did not obliterate the preexisting culture; rather, it incorporated and embedded the local customs and non-Islamic elements"; the Javanese *abangan* "practice a much more syncretic version of Islam than the more orthodox santri," and "their belief system integrates Hinduism, Buddhism and animism"; for some Indonesians "Islam is only an element in a syncretic belief system that also includes animist and Hindu-Buddhist concepts." And whole regions never became Muslim at all: Bali remains Hindu, six Papuan and Sulawesi provinces are Protestant-majority, East Nusa Tenggara is Catholic-majority, and roughly half the Batak of North Sumatra are Protestant.

This does **not** prove any doctrinal proposition about Islam. That one region converted without foreign conquest is an `outcome`-layer historical fact; it is not evidence about what Islamic scripture or law mandates regarding compulsion, which lives on a different layer and needs its own card. It does not generalize to how Islam spread elsewhere — Anatolia, North Africa, and the Iranian plateau followed different routes, and CMOS-0014 carries that variation. It does not establish where the region's Islam came from: the Gujarati/South-Indian, Arab-Hadhrami, Persian, and Chinese theses all have live advocates, the fetched record itself offers competing accounts ("One theory states that Islam arrived directly from Arabia as early as the 9th century"; "Another theory credits Sufi travelers for bringing Islam in the 12th or 13th century, either from Gujarat in India or from Persia"), and this card takes no side. It does not settle the earliest dates — the epigraphic candidates are contested and mutually inconsistent. It does not say the region is uniformly or completely Muslim. It makes no claim about mainland Southeast Asia, which was not researched here. And it is not a scoreboard: a large Muslim population is a demographic fact, not an argument that any religion is true.

## Source Anchors

### US Library of Congress country study — the load-bearing pair: no outside conquest, and local religious warfare

Country-study summary (from countrystudies.us, which reproduces the Library of Congress Federal Research Division's *Indonesia: A Country Study*):

> The decisive sentence for this card's first half: "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted." And, on the same page, the sentence that stops the romance: "states that had converted to Islam often waged war against those that adhered to the older, Hindu-Buddhist traditions." On mechanism: "Over the centuries, merchants from Arabian Sea and Indian Ocean ports and mystics and literary figures propagated the faith," and "Melaka's trading fleets brought Islam to coastal areas of the archipelago," with the study naming "the conversion of rulers to Islam in coastal Malay regions as a 'great turning point'" and describing the Javanese tradition of "nine (or ten) saints (wali in Arabic), who converted rulers through the use of supernatural powers," alongside "the Sufi tradition--a mystical branch of Islam that emphasizes the ultimate reality of God." On dating: "conversion of rulers and significant numbers of indigenous peoples to Islam apparently did not begin until around the late thirteenth century," corroborated by Marco Polo — "Landing in northern Sumatra on his way back to Europe from China in 1292, he discovered an Islamic town, Perlak" — and by epigraphy: "An inscription from a tombstone dated 1297 reveals that the first ruler of Samudra...was a Muslim." On unevenness and syncretism: "the process of Islamization there was both complex and protracted"; "Islamization proceeded more rapidly in the former than the latter" (coasts versus interior); "Among some Indonesians, Islam is only an element in a syncretic belief system that also includes animist and Hindu-Buddhist concepts."

Source: https://countrystudies.us/indonesia/5.htm

### Wikipedia, Spread of Islam in Indonesia — the mechanisms, the coercion, the dating dispute, all on one page

Wikipedia summary:

> Mechanism: "As Muslim traders settled in coastal areas, they began to assimilate with the local population"; "Many Muslims inter-married with royalties, with their descendants establishing various sultanates in Sumatra and Java"; "mystical Sufi Muslim teachers, possibly claiming supernatural powers (keramat), are thought to be a more probable agent of religious conversion"; "Organised spread of Islam is also evident by the existence of the Wali Sanga (nine holy patriarchs) who are credited for the Islamisation of Indonesia." Pace: "The spread of Islam was slow," and "the fragmentary evidence does not suggest a rolling wave of conversion through adjacent areas; rather, it suggests the process was complicated and slow." Origin dispute, unresolved on the page itself: "One theory states that Islam arrived directly from Arabia as early as the 9th century, during the time of the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates," while "Another theory credits Sufi travelers for bringing Islam in the 12th or 13th century, either from Gujarat in India or from Persia." Dating evidence, each item hedged: "The earliest legibly inscribed tombstone is dated AH 475 (AD 1082), although as it belongs to a non-Indonesian Muslim, there is doubt as to whether it was transported to Java at a later time"; "the first evidence of a Muslim dynasty is the gravestone, dated AH 696 (AD 1297), of Sultan Malik al Saleh"; "Marco Polo, on his way home from China in 1292, reported at least one Muslim town"; Ibn Battuta reported the Shafi'i school's presence "in 1346." And the force, stated on the very same page that calls the spread "generally peaceful": "Later findings of scholars say that some parts of Java, i.e. Sunda kingdom West Java and the kingdom of Majapahit on East Java was conquered by Javanese Muslims"; "The Hindu-Buddhist Sunda Kingdom of Pajajaran was conquered by Muslims in the 16th century"; "In 1520, Ali Mughayat Syah started military campaigns to dominate the northern part of Sumatra. He conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam." That internal tension is not a flaw in the source; it is the actual shape of the history, and this card reproduces both halves rather than choosing the flattering one.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spread_of_Islam_in_Indonesia

### Wikipedia, Islam in Indonesia — the social channel, the timeline, and the syncretic outcome

Wikipedia summary:

> "Islam was introduced through Arab Muslim traders, and then the missionary activity by scholars." The elite channel: "the spread of Islam among the ruling class was precipitated as Muslim traders married the local women, with some of the wealthier traders marrying into the elite ruling families," and "After local rulers and the royals had adopted Islam, their subjects mirrored their conversion." Sufism: "the strong presence of Sufism has been considered a major enabler of this syncretism between Islam and other religions." Timeline: "There is evidence of Arab Muslim traders entering Indonesia as early as the 8th century. However, it was not until the end of the 13th century that the spread of Islam began"; "By the end of the 13th century, Islam had been established in Northern Sumatra"; "the limited evidence suggests that it accelerated in the 15th century." Outcome: "Islam did not obliterate the preexisting culture; rather, it incorporated and embedded the local customs and non-Islamic elements among rules and arts, and reframed them as the Islamic traditions," and "Abangan was considered an indigenous blend of native and Hindu-Buddhist beliefs with Islamic practices." Missionary provenance, plural rather than singular: "The missionaries had originated from several countries and regions, initially from India (i.e. Gujarat) and Southeast Asia (i.e. Champa, Vietnam), and later from the southern Arabian Peninsula (i.e. Hadhramaut, Yemen)." Java's turn: "This last Hindu kingdom in Java fell under the rising power of the Islamized Sultanate of Demak in the 1520s."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Indonesia

### Wikipedia, Samudera Pasai Sultanate — the earliest attested Muslim dynasty, and why its date is not settled

Wikipedia summary:

> Commercial origin, stated as probability rather than fact: "The establishment of the first Muslim centres in Indonesia was probably a result of commercial circumstances." Founding tradition: "The kingdom was believed to have been founded by Merah Silu, who later converted to Islam and adopted the name Malik ul Salih, in the year 1267 CE." Epigraphy: "A 1297 royal tomb at Samudra is inscribed entirely in Arabic," and "The discovery of the tomb of Sultan Malik as-Saleh (696 H or 1267 AD), was referred to by historians as a sign that Islam had entered the Archipelago around the 13th century." Outside eyewitnesses: "Marco Polo spent five months here, he had Ferlec, Basma, and Samara (Samudera) mentioned in his travel story," and "Ibn Battuta on his way to China stayed 15 days at Samudera," dated on this page to "the year 1345." Frontier status: "At that time Samudera Pasai was the end of Dar al-Islam for no territory east of this was ruled by a Muslim ruler." **Dating flag, recorded not resolved:** this page renders AH 696 as "1267 AD" in one sentence while dating the same royal tomb to 1297 in another; AH 696 corresponds to 1296-97 CE, which is what the Spread-of-Islam page gives ("AH 696 (AD 1297)"). This card therefore deploys no settled founding year for Pasai, and treats 1267 (traditional founding) and 1297 (tombstone) as two different, unreconciled data points.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samudera_Pasai_Sultanate

### Wikipedia, Islam in Malaysia — the peninsula's version: ports first, hinterland centuries later

Wikipedia summary:

> "Islam was introduced to Malaysia by traders arriving from Persia, Arabia, China and the Indian subcontinent," and "It became firmly established in the 15th century." Origin, contested here too: "The main theory of the Islamization of the area comes either from the Indian subcontinent (either Gujarat or South India) or the Arab-Persian lands and this most likely started in the 12th century." The marriage-and-ruler mechanism in one sentence: "Sultan Megat Iskandar Shah, previously known as Parameswara prior to his conversion, is the first Sultan of Melaka in the early 15th century. He converted to Islam after marrying a princess from Pasai." The page's own characterization — which this card reports but does not adopt as house wording: "The religion was adopted peacefully by the people of the coastal trading ports in modern-day Malaysia and Indonesia, absorbing rather than conquering existing beliefs." The pace: "Islam gradually spread from coastal ports to the hinterland, by the 17th century the majority of people in the Malay Peninsula had converted to Islam." Contested epigraphy: the Terengganu inscription stone "is dated to either the year 1303 or 1383," and "The discovery of a Muslim tombstone in Pahang, which has been dated to 419 AH (1028 CE), suggests Islam's presence in the Malay world much earlier" — the page's own verb is "suggests." Persisting substrate: "Syncretist Islam with elements of Shamanism is still common in rural areas."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_Malaysia

### Wikipedia, Demak Sultanate — the indigenous conquest half, in detail

Wikipedia summary:

> Demak began as "A port fief to the Hindu-Buddhist Majapahit kingdom thought to have been founded in the last quarter of the 15th century," and "It was the only Majapahit fiefdom with a Muslim ruler." It seceded: "Demak decided it was no longer obliged to send tribute to the Majapahit central court and declared its independence." Then it fought: "Between 1513 and 1518 Demak waged war against Patih Udara of Daha, the successor state of Majapahit"; "Demak managed to consolidate its power by defeating Daha in 1518"; and under Trenggana it "conquered the Hindu-based resistance in Central Java and during his second campaign, conquered the last Javanese Hindu-Buddhist state, the largely defunct remnants of Majapahit." The religious establishment was fused to the state: "He appointed members of Wali Songo as advisors within his new government: Sunan Kudus as qadi (great judge of religious law), Sunan Giri as mufti, and Sunan Kalijaga as imam and advisor." Honesty note recorded from the fetch: this page does **not** itself assert that Majapahit fell *because of* Islamization, and this card does not claim it did — the sequence is documented; the causal reading is not.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demak_Sultanate

### Wikipedia, Aceh Sultanate — corroborating the campaigns, and NOT corroborating the forced-conversion sentence

Wikipedia summary:

> "Ali Mughayat Syah, who began campaigns to extend his control over northern Sumatra and the Indonesia in 1520," and "His conquests included Deli, Pedir, and Pasai, and he attacked Aru." Aceh's scholarly stature: "It was called the 'porch of Mecca', and became a center of Islamic scholarship, where the Qur'an and other Islamic texts were translated into Malay." **Single-source flag, stated plainly:** this page corroborates the 1520 campaigns and the conquests, but it contains no statement that Aceh imposed Islam on conquered peoples. The words "submitted the people to Islam" rest on the Spread-of-Islam-in-Indonesia page alone. The campaigns are double-sourced; the forced-Islamization gloss is single-sourced, and is deployed with that hedge attached.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aceh_Sultanate

### Wikipedia, Sultanate of Sulu and Islam in the Philippines — the coercive economy, and the comparative control case

Wikipedia summaries:

> Sulu's arrival story is the same commercial-and-Sufi pattern: "Islamic historian Cesar Adib Majul argues that Islam was introduced to the Sulu Archipelago in the late 14th century by Chinese and Arab merchants and missionaries from Ming China"; "In 1380 CE, Karim ul-Makhdum arrived in Simunul island from Malacca, again with Arab traders"; the seven missionaries "were Sunni Sufi scholars from the Ba 'Alawi sada of Yemen." But the same sultanate ran a slave economy: "The Sulu sultanate became notorious for its so-called 'Moro Raids' or acts of piracy on Spanish settlements in the Visayan areas in Northern Philippines and from coastal and river villages in North Borneo (Sabah), with the aim of capturing natives to be sold at slave markets," and "It is estimated that as much as 50% of the population of Sulu in the 1850s were bisaya slaves and dominated the Sulu economy."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultanate_of_Sulu

> The Philippine case supplies both the reach of the commercial spread and the control case for what conquest actually looks like: "Islam reached the Philippine archipelago in the 14th century, through contact with Muslim Malay and Arab merchants along Southeast Asian trade networks"; "The first Sunni Sufi missionaries then followed in the late 14th and early 15th centuries"; "By the 15th century, half of Luzon (Northern Philippines) and the islands of Mindanao in the south had become subject to the various Muslim sultanates." Then it stopped, and the mechanism that stopped it was conquest by someone else: "After 1565, during the Spanish colonial period (1565-1898), the Spanish attacked Muslim communities in Mindanao and Sulu...the subsequent Spanish conquest led to Catholic Christianity becoming the predominant religion in most of the modern-day Philippines." Raiding is recorded on the Muslim side too — "Muslim raiders launched attacks on Spanish-controlled towns in the Visayas."

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam_in_the_Philippines

### Wikipedia, Wali Sanga — the Sufi teaching channel, with its own historiographical warning

Wikipedia summary:

> The Wali Sanga were "a group of nine da'is (Islamic missionary) that contributed to the spread of Islam in Indonesia, especially on the island of Java," and "Multiple sources and conventional wisdom agree that the Wali Sanga contributed to the propagation of Islam (but not its original introduction)" — note the parenthesis, which blocks the popular story that they *brought* Islam. Method, in the surviving attributions: Sunan Kalijaga "Used wayang kulit shadow puppets and gamelan music to convey spiritual teachings," and Sunan Bonang and Sunan Drajat "Composed songs for gamelan orchestra." The page then undercuts its own subject, which is why it belongs here: "Although referred to as a group, there is good evidence that fewer than nine were alive in the same time period," and "the term 'Wali Sanga' was created retroactively by historians, and so there was no official 'group of nine' that had membership." The Sufi-teaching channel is real; the tidy hagiography of nine saints is a later construction, and this card says so.

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wali_Sanga

### World History Connected (University of Illinois Press) — the non-Wikipedia scholarly corroboration of the mechanism

Journal-article summary (Ethan Hawkley, "Islam and Modernity: A Reconciliation through Southeast Asian History," *World History Connected* Vol. 17 No. 3):

> "As early as the eighth century, Arab and Persian Muslims traveled through Southeast Asia on their way to China." The commercial selection pressure that made ruler-conversion pay: "Muslim merchants were more likely to settle and trade their valuable wares in settlements ruled by local Islamic chieftains." Then the local initiative: "Southeast Asian elites residing in these rising Islamic trading centers, like Samudra, Aceh, Melaka, and Brunei, took the initiative to spread the religion into other settlements," and — the enumeration of channels, from an academic publisher rather than an encyclopedia — "This was done through preaching, trading partnerships, political alliances, and intermarriage." On the Sufis: "Muslim Sufis, called Shaikhs, fled to the Indian Ocean and its various ports. These Sufis on the run were among the first to preach Islam." On the syncretic outcome: "New Muslims could therefore continue affirming many of their prior beliefs even after conversion," and "Southeast Asians wove Islam into the region's political culture." Recorded from the fetch: this article does **not** characterize the spread as either peaceful or forceful, so it is used for mechanism only.

Source: https://worldhistoryconnected.press.uillinois.edu/17.3/Hawkley.html

### JUSPI (UIN Sumatera Utara) — the dating dispute conceded by a specialist journal, and a live specimen of the overclaim this card refuses

Journal abstract summary (Syifa Shofura, Muhammad Ihsan, and Siti Safira Al-Latifatuzzahra, "The Arrival of Islam in Indonesia: A Theory of Islam's Spread," *JUSPI (Jurnal Sejarah Peradaban Islam)* Vol 8, No 1, 2024):

> The concession that justifies this card's refusal to date the first conversions: "The arrival of Islam in the archipelago is currently still being debated, due to the lack of reliable scientific data and evidence, as well as the one-sided attitude of various theories." And then, in the same abstract, the exact wording this card treats as unsafe: "one thing that is certain from all of these theories is that Islam developed in the archipelago peacefully, unlike Christianity which had a goal." That sentence is an unhedged comparative absolute in a published journal abstract, and it is cited here as **documentation of how the 'peaceful spread' topos circulates in the literature** — the same use Card 010 makes of an unhedged library figure — not as support for it. A source that concedes the evidence is thin and then declares one conclusion "certain" is exactly the kind of anchor a hostile reader will find first; better we flag it than they do.

Source: https://jurnal.uinsu.ac.id/index.php/juspi/article/view/19700

## Evidence Grade

**B** — for the claim as scoped in the frontmatter. Twelve successful live fetches across four distinct publishers (Wikipedia x10 articles, the US Library of Congress country-study text via countrystudies.us, *World History Connected* / University of Illinois Press, and JUSPI / UIN Sumatera Utara) are mutually consistent on the load-bearing shape, with every variance recorded rather than smoothed.

Two components sit at different strengths, and honesty requires splitting them:

- **A-strength (near-uncontested), though this tier cannot certify A:** that no outside Muslim empire conquered maritime Southeast Asia into Islam — stated flatly by the Library of Congress country study, implied by every other fetched account, and contradicted by none of them; and the present-day demographic result (Indonesia the largest national Muslim population, about 249.8 million).
- **B (this card's ceiling):** the mechanism synthesis — commerce, ruler conversion, intermarriage, Sufi teaching — together with the coercion correction and the chronology. The *shape* is consensus across all four publishers; the *weighting* of the channels, the origin of the region's Islam, and the dating of the earliest conversions are genuinely disputed, and the last two are scoped out of the graded claim entirely.

**Refuter-pass record (2026-08-02).** All three lenses were run in-context before drafting, and each forced changes that are visible in the card:

- *Primary-source / empirical.* Forced four corrections. (1) Scope: the sources support **maritime/insular** Southeast Asia, not "Southeast Asia" — mainland Southeast Asia was not researched here and is excluded from the claim. (2) The Wali Sanga cannot be deployed as a documented group of nine ("created retroactively by historians"; "fewer than nine were alive in the same time period"), only as the Sufi-teaching channel. (3) No first-conversion date survives: the Leran-type tombstone of AH 475/1082 is doubted as possibly transported; the Pahang tombstone of 419 AH/1028 only "suggests"; the Terengganu stone is "either 1303 or 1383"; and the Samudera Pasai article internally renders AH 696 as both 1267 and 1297. The card therefore dates the *process*, not the *origin*. (4) "The region converted" is false as stated — Bali, six Papuan/Sulawesi provinces, East Nusa Tenggara, and half the Batak are not Muslim.
- *Hostile historian.* Steelman run at full strength: *this is a sanitised story; Islamization in the archipelago involved conquest, slave raiding, and forced conversion of interior peoples, and the "peaceful trade" narrative is a modern nationalist construction.* Three of its four charges landed and are now inside the Claim rather than the caveats: the indigenous conquests (Demak, Pajajaran, Aceh), the explicit forced-Islamization sentence about Daya, and the Sulu slave economy at 50% of the population. The fourth charge — that the peaceful-spread narrative is specifically a *modern nationalist* construction — is **identified but not verified**: it surfaced only in search-result summaries, no fetched page states it, and it is therefore not asserted anywhere in this card. What the fetched record *does* support is weaker and stated instead: the "peaceful" framing circulates as an unhedged declaration in the literature (the JUSPI abstract), sometimes on the very same page as the counterevidence (the Spread-of-Islam article).
- *Category collapse.* Forced the firewalls: *how Islam spread here* is not *how Islam spreads* (CMOS-0014 carries the variation; Anatolia went differently); *no external conquest* is not *no coercion*; a *ruler's* conversion is not a *population's* belief (which is what the abangan material is doing in this card); an `outcome`-layer regional fact is not a `quran`/`prophetic`-layer doctrinal proof; and the Spanish conquest of the northern Philippines is admissible only as a symmetry check on selective standards, never as a "they did it too" excuse.

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> The largest Muslim population on earth is Indonesia, and no caliphal army — Arab, Persian, Turkish or Mughal — ever crossed the sea to take it. So who converted it, and with what? And then the harder half, which the comfortable version of this story leaves out: if the answer is only "merchants and mystics," why did the sultan of Aceh march on Daya in 1520 and, in the record's own words, submit its people to Islam?

## S-Tier Lines

> The largest Muslim population on earth was not conquered into Islam. It arrived on merchant ships, entered through royal marriages, and was taught by mystics who worked in shadow-puppet theatre and gamelan music.

> A US Library of Congress country study puts both halves in two sentences: "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted" — and "states that had converted to Islam often waged war against those that adhered to the older, Hindu-Buddhist traditions." Cite them together or don't cite them at all.

> Not conquered from outside is not the same as converted without force. Once a Javanese or Sumatran ruler turned Muslim, his armies were Muslim armies — Demak took the last Hindu-Buddhist state in Java, and in 1520 Aceh "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam."

> This was diffusion, not a switch. Four centuries in, Bali is still Hindu, the Papuan provinces are Protestant, East Nusa Tenggara is Catholic, and about half the Batak of North Sumatra are Protestant. A story that ends "and then everyone converted" is not this story.

> It tells you how Islam travelled in the Indian Ocean world. It does not tell you what Islam commands — that is a different question, on different evidence, and anyone who slides from one to the other has left history behind.

## Content Angles

### 1. One-line post

> No caliph's army ever crossed the Java Sea. The country with the largest Muslim population on earth got there by trade routes, royal marriages and Sufi teachers over four centuries — and then, once the local sultans were Muslim, by their own wars too. Both halves, or neither.

### 2. Myth-correction hook (breaks both cartoons at once)

> Two stories about how Southeast Asia became Muslim, both wrong. One: "spread by the sword" — but no outside Muslim empire ever conquered the archipelago, and a Library of Congress country study says so outright. Two: "spread peacefully" — but Demak conquered the last Hindu-Buddhist kingdom in Java, Pajajaran fell to Muslim armies in the 16th century, Aceh's sultan in 1520 conquered Daya and submitted its people to Islam, and the sultanate of Sulu ran a slave economy in which roughly half its 1850s population were captives. The accurate sentence is narrower and much harder to knock over: Islam was not carried into maritime Southeast Asia by foreign conquest — and once it was there, local rulers used force like local rulers everywhere.

### 3. Debate-reply form

> Take the strongest version of your claim and I will still hand you the counterevidence myself. Yes: Aceh conquered Daya and submitted its people to Islam. Yes: Demak destroyed the last Hindu-Buddhist state in Java. Yes: Sulu was a slave-raiding polity. I am not selling you merchants and mystics with the ugly parts removed. What I am telling you is a different and more specific thing — that no Arab, Persian, Turkish or Mughal army conquered this region, that Islam arrived here through commerce, marriage, courts and teachers, and that the coercion which followed was indigenous, dynastic, and downstream of a conversion that was already under way. And one more limit, before you make me defend it: none of this is an argument about doctrine. A region's history is not a religion's rulebook.

## Honest Caveats

1. **Do not say the spread was "peaceful."** That is the sentence that loses the exchange, and one Aceh citation ends it. Two of this card's own sources use versions of it — a Wikipedia page says the spread "was achieved through generally peaceful means" on the same page where Ali Mughayat Syah "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam," and a journal abstract declares it "certain" that "Islam developed in the archipelago peacefully" two sentences after conceding "the lack of reliable scientific data and evidence." Report those; do not repeat them. The defensible claim is the absence of *external conquest*.
2. **Local Islamization did involve conquest and coercion.** Demak "conquered the last Javanese Hindu-Buddhist state"; the Sunda kingdom of Pajajaran "was conquered by Muslims in the 16th century"; Aceh campaigned across northern Sumatra from 1520. The Library of Congress country study generalizes it: converted states "often waged war against those that adhered to the older, Hindu-Buddhist traditions." Say this inside the claim, not after it.
3. **The forced-conversion sentence is single-sourced.** "He conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam" appears on the Spread-of-Islam-in-Indonesia page; the dedicated Aceh Sultanate page corroborates the 1520 campaigns and the conquests but says nothing about imposing Islam. Deploy the conquest as double-sourced and the forced-Islamization gloss with the hedge attached.
4. **Muslim polities here ran slave economies.** The Sulu sultanate's raids captured people "to be sold at slave markets," and "as much as 50% of the population of Sulu in the 1850s were bisaya slaves." This is not a footnote to the region's Islamic history; it is part of it, and a defense that omitted it would deserve to lose.
5. **Ruler conversion is a political act, not a population's belief.** "After local rulers and the royals had adopted Islam, their subjects mirrored their conversion" describes a top-down cascade with obvious social pressure in it. Popular Islamization was slower, patchier, and syncretic — which is exactly what the abangan material documents. Do not read a sultan's conversion as a people's.
6. **Conversion was syncretic and stayed that way.** "Islam did not obliterate the preexisting culture"; abangan belief "integrates Hinduism, Buddhism and animism"; for some Indonesians Islam "is only an element in a syncretic belief system." Present this as the actual texture of the history — neither as a defect nor, in the opposite error, as proof of some uniquely tolerant essence.
7. **Large parts of the region did not convert.** Bali is Hindu; six provinces across Papua and North Sulawesi are Protestant-majority; East Nusa Tenggara has a Catholic plurality; roughly half the Batak of North Sumatra are Protestant. Never let "maritime Southeast Asia became Muslim" harden into "everyone converted."
8. **The origin question is open, and this card takes no side.** Gujarati/South-Indian, Arab-Hadhrami, Persian, and Chinese theses all appear in the fetched record, sometimes within a single source. Naming a winner here would be inventing a consensus that does not exist — and the older scholarly quarrel about it (Snouck Hurgronje versus his critics) surfaced only in search summaries this session and is deliberately not cited anywhere in this card, because no fetched page carried it.
9. **The earliest dates are contested and internally inconsistent in the sources.** The AH 475/1082 tombstone may have been transported; the Pahang 419 AH/1028 stone only "suggests"; the Terengganu stone is "either 1303 or 1383"; and the Samudera Pasai article gives AH 696 as both 1267 and 1297 (AH 696 is 1296-97 CE). Date the *process* from the late 13th century, and treat every earlier marker as a disputed candidate.
10. **The Wali Sanga are a real channel wrapped in a later construction.** They "contributed to the propagation of Islam (but not its original introduction)," and the group itself was "created retroactively by historians," with "fewer than nine... alive in the same time period." Use them as evidence of Sufi teaching adapted to Javanese forms; do not narrate the hagiography as documented biography.
11. **Keep the layer firewall up, in both directions.** That a region converted without foreign conquest is an `outcome`-layer fact. It is not proof of a doctrinal proposition about compulsion, and it is not proof that Islam spreads this way in general — Anatolia and North Africa did not (see CMOS-0014). Equally, the mirror error: "Aceh forced conversions" is a fact about one sultan, not a verdict on a faith. Both slides are the same mistake running in opposite directions.
12. **Comparative symmetry is a check on selective standards, not an excuse.** The Philippines supplies the control case in the fetched record: "the subsequent Spanish conquest led to Catholic Christianity becoming the predominant religion in most of the modern-day Philippines," in the same archipelago, in the same centuries. Use it to test whether a critic applies "spread by conquest" evenly. Do not use it to excuse anything — that a rival did worse has never made a wrong right, and reaching for it concedes that you have run out of evidence.
13. **Closure is reconnaissance.** No chronicle, no travel account, no inscription and no specialist monograph was read in the original. Say "on the best available reconstruction," not "the record proves."

## Unsafe Wording to Avoid

- "Islam spread peacefully in Indonesia" as an unqualified line. (The single most likely sentence to be destroyed on camera; Aceh, Demak, Pajajaran and Sulu are one search away.)
- "No force was ever used" / "not a drop of blood was shed." (Flatly false — local Muslim dynasties conquered Hindu-Buddhist states and, in at least one recorded case, submitted a conquered people to Islam.)
- "No Muslim army ever conquered Indonesia." (True of outside armies, false of local ones — and the version currently sitting in CMOS-0014. Use "not conquered into Islam from outside.")
- "This proves Islam is a religion of peace" / "this proves that Islam is inherently non-coercive." (A doctrinal conclusion smuggled out of a demographic fact — the exact category collapse this card exists to block.)
- "Only Islam spread without conquest" / "no other religion spread by trade." (Comparative overclaims that invert the very error being corrected.)
- "Indonesia was converted by Arab missionaries." (The origin question is genuinely open; the fetched record offers Gujarati, South Indian, Persian, Chinese and Hadhrami routes.)
- "Islam was the first ever religion to reach the archipelago without an army." (An unhedged priority claim this card cannot support and does not need.)
- "The Wali Songo converted Java." (They are credited with propagation "but not its original introduction," and the group of nine is a retrospective construction.)
- "Southeast Asia became Muslim." (Scope error twice over: this card covers the maritime zone only, and large parts of even that zone are not Muslim.)
- "The largest Muslim population on earth proves Islam's truth." (A demographic fact is not a theological argument, in any direction.)

## Safe Wording

> Maritime Southeast Asia — Indonesia, the Malay peninsula, Brunei, the southern Philippines — became predominantly Muslim without being conquered by any outside Muslim empire. A US Library of Congress country study states it plainly: "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted." What carried Islam there instead were the trade routes of the Indian Ocean and South China Sea: Muslim merchants who settled in the ports, married into local ruling families, and made Muslim-ruled harbours the profitable ones; local rulers who converted, after which "their subjects mirrored their conversion"; and Sufi teachers who worked through Javanese forms, one of them credited with using shadow-puppet theatre and gamelan music to teach. It was slow and patchy — traders are attested from the 8th century but the spread itself begins around the end of the 13th, accelerates in the 15th, and does not reach a Malay-peninsula majority until the 17th; the evidence "does not suggest a rolling wave of conversion" but a process that was "complicated and slow," faster on the coasts than inland. Today Indonesia holds the world's largest national Muslim population, roughly 249.8 million people, about an eighth of all Muslims. But the absence of an outside conquest did not mean the absence of force, and the honest version says so in the same breath: once local dynasties became Muslim, their wars became Muslim wars — Demak conquered the last Hindu-Buddhist state in Java, the Sunda kingdom of Pajajaran fell to Muslim armies in the 16th century, Aceh's Ali Mughayat Syah in 1520 "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam," and the sultanate of Sulu ran a raiding economy in which about half its mid-19th-century population were captives. Conversion was also syncretic and incomplete: Javanese abangan practice "integrates Hinduism, Buddhism and animism," and Bali, the Papuan provinces, East Nusa Tenggara and much of the Batak highlands are not Muslim at all. The claim that survives all of this is narrow and hard to dislodge: Islam was not carried into maritime Southeast Asia by foreign conquest — it came with commerce, courts, marriages and teachers, over centuries — and what happened afterwards, including the coercion, was local. That is a fact about one region's history. It is not an argument about what any religion teaches.

## Counterattack Simulation

### Counter: "This is a sanitised nationalist story. Islamization in the archipelago meant conquest, slave raiding, and the forced conversion of interior peoples."

Response:

> Three-quarters of that is correct, and you did not have to tell me — it is in the claim, not the footnotes. Demak conquered the last Javanese Hindu-Buddhist state. Pajajaran "was conquered by Muslims in the 16th century." Aceh's sultan in 1520 "conquered Daya, and submitted the people to Islam" — the plainest forced-Islamization sentence in the fetched record, and I am the one quoting it. Sulu raided for captives and "as much as 50% of the population of Sulu in the 1850s were bisaya slaves." A Library of Congress country study generalizes the pattern: converted states "often waged war against those that adhered to the older, Hindu-Buddhist traditions." So take the concession in full. Now notice what none of it touches. The proposition is not "nobody used force"; it is that **no outside Muslim empire conquered this region into Islam** — and the same country study says so in the sentence before: "The archipelago was not invaded by outsiders and forcibly converted." Every conquest you can name here is *indigenous* and *downstream*: a Javanese or Sumatran dynasty that was already Muslim, fighting its neighbours the way Javanese and Sumatran dynasties always fought their neighbours. That is a different causal story from "Islam arrived at swordpoint," and it is the only one the evidence carries. On your fourth charge — that the peaceful-trade narrative is a modern nationalist construction — I looked and could not source it from any page I actually read, so I will not assert it and I will not pretend to refute it. What I can show you is weaker and stranger: the "peaceful" framing circulates as an unhedged declaration in the literature, sometimes on the very page that also records Aceh conquering Daya. If your point is that the comfortable version is doing ideological work, I am not going to fight you on that. I am refusing to deploy the comfortable version.

### Counter: "A king converts and his subjects follow. That is coercion with better manners — you have just moved the sword up one level."

Response:

> This is the strongest counter and it deserves a real concession. "After local rulers and the royals had adopted Islam, their subjects mirrored their conversion" describes a top-down cascade, and there is no world in which a subject population's religion is a free market when the palace has already chosen. Political gravity was the water everyone swam in. Concede it. But watch what the equation "top-down equals coercion" then has to explain away. First, why the rulers converted at all: they were not being invaded, they were competing for shipping. Merchants "were more likely to settle and trade their valuable wares in settlements ruled by local Islamic chieftains" — the incentive ran through the harbour, not the garrison. Second, the pace. If a ruler's decree converted a population, the process would be fast; instead it is "complicated and slow," it moves "more rapidly" on the coast than inland, and the Malay peninsula does not reach a majority until the 17th century — centuries after Melaka's sultan converted. Decrees do not take three hundred years to work. Third, the residue. If the cascade were coercive at the level you need, you would not get abangan Islam "integrating Hinduism, Buddhism and animism" surviving for centuries in the Javanese heartland, nor an entire Hindu island next door. So: social pressure, yes, thoroughly conceded. Political dominance as background condition, conceded. A sword one level up, declined — because the mechanism you are describing left exactly the fingerprints a slow, negotiated, partial conversion leaves, and none of the ones a coerced conversion leaves.

### Counter: "If Islam arrived by trade and stayed syncretic, it barely arrived at all — half of Java is not really Muslim anyway."

Response:

> Careful, because this counter is aimed at the wrong target and it hits an ugly one. The card's claim is about how a population became Muslim, not about whose Islam is authentic — and "abangan Javanese are not really Muslim" is a takfir argument, not a historical one. It is also the mirror of the smear it thinks it is defeating: one side says the sword made them Muslim, the other says nothing did. What the record shows is a third thing, and it is the normal shape of religious change everywhere: Islam "did not obliterate the preexisting culture; rather, it incorporated and embedded the local customs and non-Islamic elements," and "New Muslims could therefore continue affirming many of their prior beliefs even after conversion." That is what conversion at scale looks like when it is not imposed by an occupying army — layered, negotiated, uneven, and long. Note also what the counter's own premise concedes: if Javanese Islam is syncretic *because* nobody forced a clean break, that is evidence for this card's mechanism, not against it. And the counter overshoots its facts: 87% of Indonesians are Muslim, Sumatra's Islam is described in the sources as "relatively orthodox," and Aceh is called "the porch of Mecca," a centre of Islamic scholarship where the Qur'an was translated into Malay. A tradition that produced its own jurists, its own scholarly networks, and its own translated canon did not "barely arrive."

### Counter (aimed at our own side): "Fine — no conquest here. So Islam spreads peacefully, and the sword narrative is exposed as a lie."

Response:

> No, and this is the error I most want to block, because it is ours and it will cost more than the hostile version. This card is one region. Islam reached Anatolia, North Africa, Iran and Sind by routes that included conquest, and CMOS-0014 documents that expansion honestly — fast conquest of *territory*, slow and mostly non-coerced conversion of *populations*, under real fiscal and legal pressure. Maritime Southeast Asia is the case where the conquest half is simply absent, which makes it evidentially valuable precisely because it is *not* the general pattern. Generalize from it and you have built the mirror image of the smear: a single region promoted to a law. Worse, the generalization tries to smuggle a doctrinal conclusion out of a demographic fact — "they converted without conquest, therefore the religion forbids compulsion." That inference does not run. What Islamic scripture and law say about compulsion is a separate question, on separate evidence, needing its own card, and it would remain the same question if every conversion in the archipelago had been at swordpoint. Hold the line where the evidence is: **not conquered into Islam from outside** — a specific, sourced, regional claim that survives cross-examination, which is worth more than a sweeping one that does not.
