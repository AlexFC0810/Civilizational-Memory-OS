## Claim

Islamic civilization did not abolish slavery from within. Slaveholding and long-distance slave trading persisted across Muslim societies for more than a millennium — the fetched record dates slavery in the Muslim world "from the seventh century to the present day," the trans-Saharan traffic "from the 7th century until the early-to-mid-20th century," and the Red Sea traffic "from antiquity until the mid-20th century," ending only "in the 1960s, when slavery in Saudi Arabia and Yemen were abolished." Legal abolition arrived late, unevenly, and substantially under external and colonial pressure, and some states abolished only in the twentieth century: Egypt in 1895, Afghanistan in 1921, Iran in 1929, Bahrain in 1937, Kuwait in 1949, Qatar in 1952, Saudi Arabia and Yemen in 1962, Oman in 1970, and Mauritania — "the last country in the world to officially abolish slavery" — in 1981, by presidential decree that carried no criminal penalty, so that owning a human being did not become a prosecutable offence there until 2007, "under international pressure," with exactly one person prosecuted as of January 2011.

The tradition was not empty of resources against this, and that is what makes the verdict worse rather than better. The Qur'an names spending "for freeing captives" among the marks of righteousness (2:177); it makes "˹freeing˺ slaves" one of the fixed categories of the obligatory alms-tax (9:60); it requires that a man in a particular broken oath "must free a slave before they touch each other" (58:3); it instructs that when a bondsperson "desires a contract ˹to buy their own" freedom the owner is to respond and to "give them some of Allah's wealth which He has granted you" (24:33); and on captives after battle it says "Later ˹free them either as˺ an act of grace or by ransom until the war comes to an end" (47:4). Alongside these ran the orthodox juristic rule that a free Muslim could not be enslaved. These were real, and they were used continuously for twelve centuries. They did not produce abolition. They did not even hold the line they themselves drew: the trans-Saharan traffic ran, in the fetched record's own words, "despite the orthodox Muslim jurist position that no Muslim, regardless of race, could be enslaved," and the nineteenth-century Moroccan historian Ahmad ibn Khalid an-Nasiri "bemoaned the 'unlimited enslavement of blacks' in 19th century North Africa 'where men traffic them like beasts or worse' and where the majority of slaves were Muslims who should have been exempt from slavery because of their religious status." A rule that named the protected class and then failed to protect it is not a mitigating fact; it is an aggravating one, because it establishes that the wrong was legible to the system that committed it.

Worse, the correctives and the trade were coupled. The same record that shows manumission was routine explains what routine manumission did: "the growth of internal slave populations through natural increase was insufficient to maintain slave numbers through to modern times," and "the Islamic injunctions against the enslavement of Muslims led to the massive importation of slaves from the outside." A system that keeps freeing people, and that forbids replenishing itself from within, must be fed from beyond its own borders — permanently. The mercy at the exit was paid for at the entrance, and it was paid by strangers. What that cost looked like is in the same sources: on the Sahara crossing Gustav Nachtigal "estimated that for every one slave that successfully arrived at the market three or four had either died or escaped"; eunuchs, castrated for the harem and administrative markets, were "around seven times more expensive than non-castrated males"; two thirds of Zanzibar's population is estimated to have been enslaved in the 1850s; and on the Red Sea route the pilgrimage itself became "the biggest vehicle for enslavement," traffickers moving "women and children in the guise of wives, servants and pilgrims to Hejaz."

The sharpest single case is the Zanj. Enslaved Africans were imported into the Sawad of southern Iraq "primarily for agricultural labor," put "into work camps and tasked with clearing away the nitrous topsoil," and set "to work in the salt flats of the Sawad, especially in the area around Basra," where "both the working and living conditions of the Zanj were considered to be extremely miserable." In September 869 they rose, and the war ran until the fall of al-Mukhtara in August 883 — fourteen years. And here is the thing this card exists to say: almost everything known about them reaches us through people who were not them. "Much of the current knowledge of the Zanj Rebellion comes from the historian al-Tabari's work History of the Prophets and Kings" — al-Tabari, born 839 in Amol and died 923 in Baghdad, "well past seventy in the year his History was published," a private scholar at the Abbasid capital writing decades after the last of the rebels was killed. No source authored by the Zanj themselves is named anywhere in the fetched record. Their numbers survive only as chroniclers' figures that "modern historians" call "gross exaggerations." A civilization that preserved al-Tabari across eleven centuries preserved not one line in which the people it worked in the salt flats speak for themselves.

Abolition, when it came, mostly came from outside the tradition's own initiative — and resistance to it was voiced in religious terms *locally*, while the central religious authority, when finally asked, permitted the trade ban but expressly protected existing ownership. (Corrected by the CoS at integration, 2026-08-03: an earlier draft of this sentence read "where it was resisted, it was resisted in religious terms," which the card's own evidence does not support — the Şeyhülislam ruled *for* the firman's compatibility with sharia. The accurate and still-damning finding is the shape of that accommodation, not blanket clerical resistance.) In October 1854 the Ottoman sultan prohibited the trade in Circassian and Georgian slaves "after pressure from Great Britain and France"; the reading of that firman in the Hijaz produced "the ulema of condemning it as contrary to sharia," notables condemned it as a "concession to Europeans," and the province rose in the Hejaz rebellion of 1854–1856. The Şeyhülislam had to rule on 11 January 1856 that "the firman did not violate sharia" and that "while it did prohibit the slave trade it did not prohibit slavery itself and did not threaten the slaveowners possession of their human property." When "British pressure resulted in the Ottoman Sultan issuing a firman (decree) that prohibited the slave trade" in 1857, that decree "excluded the Hejaz Province, which was the main destination of the Red Sea slave trade," precisely because the earlier prohibition "had already caused the Hejaz rebellion" — the holy province was carved out of an anti-slavery decree by religious objection — and the Firman of 1857 "was never enforced in practice." The 1880 Anglo-Ottoman Convention gave Britain "the right to stop and control all ships suspected of trafficking slaves on Ottoman waters," and the fetched summary of Ottoman policy is unsparing: the authorities "officially opposed slavery for the sake of diplomacy in their foreign policy, but tolerated slavery and slave trade in the provinces of the Empire in their internal policy." In Zanzibar the sultan signed away the mainland import trade in the 1873 Frere treaty under a naval patrol; "In 1897 the British forced the Sultan to abolish slavery in Zanzibar by declaring that it lacked legal status"; and "In 1909, the British finally forced the sultan to include the concubines in the abolition, which signified the final and actual abolition of slavery in Zanzibar" — concubinage was the last thing surrendered, and it was surrendered under force. In Mauritania the French "declared an end to slavery in Mauritania in 1905, but did little to enforce that ban," and the 2007 criminalisation came "under international pressure." Saudi Arabia's 1962 abolition followed "growing international pressure from the United Nations" and a Kennedy-administration demand for reforms "heavily directed against slavery."

One case cuts the other way, and honesty requires it be stated at full strength rather than buried. Tunisia abolished slavery by its own Muslim sovereign's decree, with its own jurists' authority, before any European administration was in place. Ahmad Bey "closed the slave market of Tunis in August and declared in December 1842 that everyone born in the country would thereafter be free," and "The complete abolition of slavery throughout the country was declared in a decree of 23 January 1846" — and crucially, "To alleviate discontent, Ahmed obtained fatwas from the ulama beforehand from the Bach-mufti Sidi Brahim Riahi, which forbade slavery, categorically and without any precedent in the Arab Muslim world." An internal Islamic abolitionist argument was therefore available, was made, and was ruled valid by a mufti in 1841. The breaker evidence against the cleanest version of that story belongs in the same breath: the sequence began when "Ahmed I Bey had an interview with Thomas Reade who advised him to ban the slave trade" — the British consul — and the abolition "was rejected... at Djerba, among the Bedouins, and among the peasants who required a cheap and obedient workforce," which is why the French had to announce a second abolition by decree in 1890. Tunisia does not redeem the record. It indicts it: it removes the defence that the argument could not have been made, and leaves the question of why, once made and once ratified by a mufti, no other Muslim state followed for almost fifty years.

This does **not** establish that Islamic scripture commands or mandates enslavement — that is a `quran`-layer claim this card does not touch and does not support, and the same fetched source that records the failure states flatly of the Qur'an: "The freeing of slaves is encouraged as an act of benevolence, and expiation of sins." It does not establish that slavery in Muslim societies was the same institution as Atlantic racial chattel slavery; those were different systems with different legal structures, different demographic profiles, and different afterlives, and collapsing them is a factual error whichever direction it is made in. It does not establish anything about any other civilization: comparative history is mentioned here once, and only to block a claim this card would otherwise be misread as making — that slaveholding was a uniquely Islamic evil, which it was not, and which is not the proposition on trial. Nothing about that comparison reduces the verdict, and any deployment that uses it to do so has inverted the card. It does not establish a total number for any of these trades: two fetched pages give irreconcilable totals. It does not establish that the Zanj rebellion was a slave revolt in the strict sense — modern historians dispute the rebel force's composition — nor that it aimed at abolishing slavery, which no fetched source states. And it does not establish the causal weighting between external and internal drivers of abolition, which remains live scholarly dispute; this card grades the timing, the sequence, and the recorded pressure, not the counterfactual.

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> For more than a thousand years Muslim societies bought, held, worked, and shipped human beings — and the tradition's own instruments against it were in continuous use the whole time. Freeing a captive is named in the Qur'an as a mark of righteousness, funded as one of the fixed categories of the alms-tax, imposed as expiation, and made claimable by contract; and the orthodox jurists held that no free Muslim of any race could be enslaved. Those instruments did not end the institution, and by the nineteenth century a Moroccan Muslim historian was writing that most of those being trafficked across the Sahara were themselves Muslims who should have been exempt. When the Ottoman sultan banned the Circassian slave trade in 1854 under European pressure, the ulema of Mecca condemned the ban as contrary to sharia, the Hijaz rose in revolt, and the 1857 decree against the African slave trade had to exempt the holy province — then went unenforced anyway. Meanwhile Ahmad Bey of Tunis obtained fatwas from his own mufti forbidding slavery categorically and abolished it outright on 23 January 1846, which proves the argument was available inside the tradition. So the question is not whether Islam could produce an abolitionist case. It did. The question is why, having produced it, this civilization did not act on it — and why Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Oman, and Mauritania needed the twentieth century, and Mauritania the twenty-first, to finish it.

## Honest Caveats

1. **Do not lead with, or lean on, "other civilizations did it too."** It is the exact move this repo exists to detect in others, and using it here would forfeit the card's entire purpose. 2. **Do not offer the manumission architecture as mitigation.** State it as what it is: an internal corrective that existed, was funded, was scripturally mandated, was used continuously, and did not succeed. The strongest version of this discipline is the coupling: because the freed could not be replaced from within, the exits generated permanent demand at the entrance.
3. **Do not soften the doctrinal violation into a doctrinal defence.** The rule that no free Muslim could be enslaved is not evidence of a working protection; the fetched record says the traffic ran "despite" it and that most of the nineteenth-century North African trafficked were Muslims who should have been exempt. The tradition was not without resources against it: the Qur'an names spending "for freeing captives" among the marks of righteousness, makes freeing slaves one of the fixed categories of the obligatory alms-tax, imposes it as expiation, and instructs owners to grant an emancipation contract to any bondsperson who asks for one and to fund it; and the orthodox jurists held that no free Muslim of any race could be enslaved. Those resources were real, and they were used continuously for twelve centuries. They did not end the institution — and they did not even hold the line they drew, because the record of the trans-Saharan trade states that it ran "despite the orthodox Muslim jurist position that no Muslim, regardless of race, could be enslaved," and a nineteenth-century Moroccan Muslim historian complained that most of those being trafficked were Muslims who should have been exempt. Worse, the correctives and the traffic were coupled: because a population that is continually freed and cannot be replenished from within must be fed from outside, the same injunctions "led to the massive importation of slaves from the outside." When abolition came, it came late and largely under outside pressure. The Ottoman ban on the Circassian trade in 1854 drew condemnation from ulema in Mecca as contrary to sharia and helped set off a revolt in the Hijaz — though the Şeyhülislam himself later ruled the firman *did not* violate sharia, and the shape of that ruling is the real indictment: it permitted the ban on the trade precisely because it "did not prohibit slavery itself and did not threaten the slaveowners possession of their human property." The 1857 prohibition of the African slave trade then exempted the holy province — and went unenforced anyway. Britain forced Zanzibar's sultan to abolish slavery in 1897 and to extend it to concubines in 1909. Egypt abolished in 1895, Iran in 1929, Saudi Arabia and Yemen in 1962, Oman in 1970, and Mauritania — the last country in the world to do so — in 1981, with criminal penalties only in 2007 and one prosecution by 2011. There is one real counter-case and it deserves to be named at full strength: in Tunisia, Ahmad Bey obtained fatwas from his own Bach-mufti forbidding slavery categorically, closed the slave market of Tunis in 1841, freed everyone born in the country in 1842, and abolished slavery by decree on 23 January 1846. So the abolitionist argument was available inside the tradition, was made, and was ratified by a jurist — and almost no one else made it for another fifty years. I am not going to tell you standards were different, or that other people did it too. The honest charge is narrower and heavier: this is a failure of Muslim societies and of the jurists who served them, measured against a standard the tradition itself supplied and did not meet. What it is not is evidence that Islamic scripture commands enslavement, or that this was the same institution as Atlantic racial chattel slavery. Those are different claims on different layers, and they need their own evidence — which this is not.

### Counter: "This is a hostile framing dressed up as scholarship. You have handed the polemicists a weapon and called it honesty."

The archive's audit made the structural version of this argument: twenty-three cards, every thesis exculpatory, each individually honest, the corpus systematically favourable. An institution whose corpus can only tilt one way has no credible verdict when it says something favourable either, because nobody can distinguish its findings from its preferences. This card is the price of the other twenty-three being believable. And notice what the honesty buys, concretely: because this card concedes the record without flinching, the Tunisian fatwas of 1841 and the Qur'anic manumission architecture land as evidence rather than as spin — which is exactly the reverse of what happens when they are led with.

### Counter: "Every civilization on earth practised slavery. Singling out Islamic civilization is bigotry with citations."

Response:

> The premise is true and the inference is a non-sequitur, and it is worth being precise about why. This card is not a comparative ranking; it does not claim that Muslim societies were worse than any other, and it explicitly refuses the claim that slaveholding was uniquely Islamic. What it claims is that *this* civilization, which is the one this archive exists to remember accurately, did not abolish slavery from within, and that its own liberation architecture failed to. That proposition would be exactly as true if every other society on earth had been worse. Now the reversal, because the selective standard runs the other way here: "everyone did it" is invoked almost exclusively by defenders and almost never by the same people when a rival civilization's record is under discussion — and this repo's stated purpose is detecting precisely that move in others. We cannot run a distortion detector that exempts its own subject. There is also a specific reason the move fails on these facts. A universal-practice defence works against a charge of unique depravity; the charge here is failure against an internal standard. The Qur'an funds manumission out of the alms-tax; the jurists ruled free Muslims unenslaveable; a Tunisian mufti ruled slavery categorically forbidden in 1841. Nobody else's record has any bearing on whether this civilization met the standard it wrote for itself.

### Counter: "So you concede it: Islam permits slavery, the Qur'an legislates for it, the Prophet owned slaves. Your own card proves the religion is a slaving religion."

Response:

> That is four claims wearing one coat, and the card grants exactly one of them. It grants that the Qur'an legislates *about* slavery, which is not in dispute and is stated in the anchors — including the sentence "the Quran doesn't abolish slavery," which is in this card because it belongs there. It does not grant the inference, and the inference is where the argument actually is. A text that names freeing captives as a mark of righteousness, allocates a fixed share of the obligatory alms-tax to buying people out of bondage, imposes manumission as a penalty for broken oaths, orders owners to grant an emancipation contract to any bondsperson who asks and to fund it from their own wealth, and directs that captives be freed by grace or ransom, is not a text whose direction of travel you can read as endorsement. Nor is a jurisprudence whose orthodox rule barred enslaving a free Muslim of any race. This card's grading is about what people did, not what the text mandates — the layer distinction is in its frontmatter, not invented for this reply — and the honest reading of the text is what makes the historical failure *legible as failure* rather than as consistency. Then there is the fact that settles it evidentially: Ahmad Bey of Tunis obtained fatwas from his own Bach-mufti in 1841 which "forbade slavery, categorically," and abolished it in 1846. If the sources mandated slavery, that ruling could not have been issued from inside the tradition by its own senior jurist. It was. Which is why the true charge against this civilization is far worse than yours — not that it was forbidden from abolishing slavery, but that it was permitted to, shown how, and did not.

### Counter: "Abolition was imposed by European empires at gunpoint. Muslims were never given the chance to reform themselves, and your card launders colonial coercion into moral credit for the colonizers."

Response:

> This is the strongest objection on the board, it has real scholarship behind it, and the card grades the causal question C precisely because of it — the specialist literature that adjudicates it is literature this card could not fetch, and that failure is logged rather than glossed. Three concessions, unforced. The pressure was coercive and often self-interested: the 1880 convention handed Britain search rights over Ottoman shipping, and Zanzibar's sultan was "forced" in 1897 and again in 1909, in a record largely written by the forcers. Colonial declarations were frequently hollow — France "declared an end to slavery in Mauritania in 1905, but did little to enforce that ban." And internal agency was real and is in this card at full strength: Tunisia's abolition, an-Nasiri's condemnation, the Mauritanian ulama's post-1980 ruling. Here is what the objection cannot carry. It requires that the tradition was *prevented* from abolishing, and the Tunisian sequence refutes that directly: an independent Muslim sovereign, his own mufti's fatwas, complete abolition on 23 January 1846 — decades before any European administration in Tunis. The chance existed, was taken once, and was not taken again for nearly fifty years. And the objection has to explain the Hijaz, where the pressure ran the other way: the ulema of Mecca condemned an anti-slave-trade decree as contrary to sharia, the province revolted, and the holy province was carved out of the 1857 prohibition as a result. That is not a civilization prevented from reforming by outsiders; that is a reform blocked from inside, in religious terms, on the record. The card's wording tracks this exactly — "substantially under external pressure" describes a documented sequence and stops there. What it will not do is convert a failure into a deprivation, because that move would leave this civilization with no agency in its own history, which is a stranger thing to defend than the failure.
