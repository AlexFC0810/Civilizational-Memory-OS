# Source Card 023: Timbuktu and the Niger-Bend Scholarly Culture (14th–16th Centuries)

Date: 2026-08-02

## Status

**Deployment status:** reconnaissance-closure draft through the mechanical gate, graded **B** — the ceiling is bolted down by six fetch failures (UNESCO World Heritage, two Library of Congress pages, the Met, Oxford Research Encyclopedia, the Berkeley WAAMD database) under the gate's fetch-failure rule, independent of adjudication tier. No chronicle edition, no translation, and no manuscript was read directly; closure stays `reconnaissance`.

**Why this card exists:** `archive/LOAD_BEARING_INDEX.md` carries a standing confession that "West Africa & the Swahili coast (Mali, Songhai, Timbuktu) are named as necessary and sourced nowhere," and that until they are carded the index "maps the Sunni-Arab/Persian core of the story." Twenty-two gated cards preceded this one and none was about Africa south of the Sahara. This card closes half of gap (2); the Swahili coast remains open.

**Layer derivation (read this before deploying):** the pre-allocation suggested `institution`. The evidence refuses it. Both fetched sources that address the question state flatly that no institutionalized centre of learning existed at Timbuktu — teaching ran through individual scholars with private students, in study circles, largely in scholars' houses. What is documented here is therefore a *scholarly culture*, not a designed institutional form, and the layer is `culture`. This is not a coverage choice; it is the finding. It also keeps the card from silently contradicting CMOS-0012 (`source-cards/012_university_madrasa_system.md`), which grades the madrasa as an `institution` precisely because it had a waqf legal constitution, salaried chairs, and student stipends — apparatus this card's sources do not attest at Timbuktu.

**Best use:** against the frame that Islamic civilization was an Arab affair to which everyone else was a recipient. The defensible, interesting claim is *production, not just reception* — indigenous jurists writing, teaching, being deported with their libraries, and being read afterwards. Pairs with CMOS-0005 (`source-cards/005_knowledge_hospitals_libraries.md`) on what the knowledge infrastructure was, and with CMOS-0012 on the university question — this card uses 012's category concession rather than repeating its argument, and must never be deployed to reverse it.

**House style:** "a culture that produced, not an institution that was chartered." Concede the university label in the first breath, concede the slaving economy in the Claim rather than a footnote, refuse every unsourced manuscript total, and let what survives carry the weight.

## Claim

Between roughly the 14th and the late 16th centuries, the city of Timbuktu on the Niger bend — under the patronage of the Mali empire and then the Songhai empire — sustained a substantial indigenous Islamic scholarly culture: Arabic-literate, locally staffed, locally financed, and locally productive. Its shape is documented, and it is not the shape the popular story gives it. Three mosques were the focal points — Djinguereber, attributed to Mansa Musa in 1325 and built 1327; Sankore, built in the 14th–15th centuries with the financial backing, per the fetched account, of "a Tuareg woman of the Aghlal tribe"; and Sidi Yahya, founded 1400 — but the teaching itself was not institutional. The fetched sources are explicit: "there is in fact no evidence for the existence of an organized educational institution in Timbuktu equivalent to a medieval or modern university," and instead "In Timbuktu scholar-teachers had their own private students, rather than working within an educational institution," with teaching in "small 'study circles' seated on the floor" and "much of the day-to-day teaching taking place in scholar's houses." What made this a scholarly culture rather than a scattering of tutors was three things the sources do attest. First, hereditary jurist lineages: the Sanhaja Berber Aqit family supplied Timbuktu's qadis and Sankore's imams across generations, and one of them, al-Qadi Aqib ibn Mahmud ibn Umar (1507/08–1583, qadi 1565–1583), rebuilt Sidi Yahya in 1569, renovated Djinguereber in 1570, built the sūq mosque in 1577/78, and rebuilt Sankore the year after — a judge with the standing and the resources to re-found the city's religious architecture. Second, a book economy: "An active trade in books between Timbuktu and other parts of the Islamic world and emperor Askia Mohammed's strong support led to the writing of thousands of manuscripts," in a city where "literacy and books were symbols of wealth, power, and blessings"; scholars accumulated private libraries, one reported "with over 1600 manuscripts." Third, and most load-bearing, indigenous authorship: Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (1556–1627), of the Aqit family, "was a prolific author and wrote more than 40 books"; al-Sa'di, born 1594, wrote the *Tarikh al-Sudan* around 1655, "the single most important primary source for the history of the Songhai Empire"; and by the 15th century, on one lecture-level account, "Timbuktu scholars were producing original works as well as compiling new derivations and commentaries on established texts." The corpus is not hypothetical: the Hill Museum & Manuscript Library reports its Mali digitization "complete, resulting in an estimated 4.35 million unique image files representing 308,000 manuscripts."

The hard half belongs in the same paragraph, not a footnote. Mali and Songhai were slave-holding, slave-trading polities, and the trans-Saharan slave trade ran through these same networks: Timbuktu "primarily gained its wealth from local gold and salt mining, in addition to the trans-Saharan slave trade"; in Songhai "at the bottom were prisoners of war and enslaved people who mainly worked in agriculture," and the empire "used slaves more consistently than their predecessors, the Ghana and Mali empires"; Ibn Battuta's caravan out of Mali "transported 600 female slaves, suggesting that slavery was a substantial part of the commercial activity of the empire"; and the single most-quoted description of Timbuktu's market lists, in one breath, "African products, gold, printed cottons, slaves and in Islamic books." Estimates for the whole trans-Saharan traffic run "from 6 to 10 million from the mid-7th century until the 20th century." The region's most celebrated jurist did not abolish this. Ahmad Baba's *Mi'raj al-Su'ud* "sought only to reform the Trans-Saharan slave trade with the goal of preventing Muslims from enslaving other Muslims"; he "was not an advocate for ending the slave trade generally" and "essentially advocated religion-based slavery instead of racial-slavery, with Muslims of any ethnicity being immune from being enslaved." Islamic learning coexisted with enslavement here as it did elsewhere in the Islamic world and outside it, and the same commerce financed both the books and the bondage. The end was violent and external: on 13 March 1591 a Moroccan expedition under Judar Pasha, sent by Ahmad al-Mansur and equipped with arquebuses and cannon against a far larger Songhai army, won at Tondibi in about two hours; Gao, Timbuktu, and Djenné were sacked, and "the looting of the three cities marked the end of the Songhai Empire as a functioning state." Scholars were then broken as a community — "In 1593, many of the city's scholars were executed or exiled for disloyalty to the new rulers," and in 1594 Ahmad Baba and others "were arrested by Moroccan troops on grounds of sedition and deported to Morocco along with their manuscript collections." He reached Morocco, remained "in Fez until the death of Ahmad al-Mansur," and returned to Timbuktu on 22 April 1608.

What this does **not** establish. It does not establish a university at Timbuktu: the "University of Sankoré" and "University of Timbuktu" are retrospective labels, and this card's own anchors reject them — the card takes CMOS-0012's category concession as binding and does not reopen it. It does not establish any total manuscript figure: the fetched record states that the manuscripts' "total number is unknown, affording only rough estimates," and the circulating totals (700,000; a million) are not sourced here and are refused as house wording. It does not establish that the surviving corpus is a medieval corpus — the corpus as described in the scholarly literature spans "between the fifteenth and twentieth centuries," so the hundreds of thousands of surviving items cannot be transferred backward onto the 14th–16th-century period this card is about. It does not establish a scale comparison with Cairo, Fez, Baghdad, or Bologna; no such comparison was fetched, and Timbuktu had less institutional apparatus than the waqf-funded Nizamiyya network, not more. It does not establish anything about West Africa as a whole: Timbuktu is one city, Djenné, Gao, and Walata are distinct centres with their own records, and the later Mauritanian and Sokoto traditions are separate again. It does not establish an unbroken tradition: 1591 broke the patronage, the security, and the community. And it establishes nothing about race or essence — the scholarly lineage at the centre of this card was Sanhaja Berber, and the claim here is geographic and civilizational (learning produced south of the Sahara), never a claim that any faith civilized any continent.

### Wikipedia: the Timbuktu Chronicles — an indigenous historiography, and a 19th-century forgery inside it

On the *Tarikh al-Fattash* — the breaker this card surfaces unprompted:

> The traditional attribution is listed as "Mahmud Kati (claimed)," and per Nobili and Mathee's 2015 analysis "the document's supposed authorship by Mahmud Kati is an invention." The text is identified as "a re-written forgery produced early in the 19th century by Nūḥ b. al-Ṭāhir but which claims Kati as its author," with Nehemia Levtzion having argued in 1971 that "Manuscript C was a forgery produced during the time of Seku Amadu in the first quarter of the 19th century." The genuine underlying chronicle was "written soon after 1664" by Ibn al-Mukhtar, a grandson of Mahmud Kati; and "the work published by Houdas and Delafosse is in fact a conflation of two separate works."

## Best Load-Bearing Question

> **In 1594 the Moroccan occupiers of Timbuktu arrested the city's scholars and deported them across the Sahara — and the fetched record says they were deported "along with their manuscript collections." One of them, Ahmad Baba, had written more than forty books; another Timbuktu scholar, al-Sa'di, would write the chronicle that remains the single most important source for the Songhai empire, and he wrote it sixty-four years after that empire fell, while employed by its conquerors. If Islamic learning in sub-Saharan Africa were only ever something received from the Arab world, what exactly did an invading army think was worth confiscating — and who was writing the books?**

## Honest Caveats

1. **Never say "the University of Sankoré" or "the University of Timbuktu" as house wording.** This card's own anchors state "there is no evidence of a centralized teaching institution such as the term university implies" and "there is in fact no evidence for the existence of an organized educational institution in Timbuktu equivalent to a medieval or modern university." Say "mosque-centred teaching circles," "study circles," or "the scholars of Sankore." Note also that the anachronism has infected the sources: the same Wikipedia article that debunks the label elsewhere calls Ahmad Baba a "chancellor" and reports an "honorary doctorate" for Mohammed Bagayogo. Report the debunking; refuse the vocabulary.
2. **Never deploy a total manuscript number.** The fetched record: "Most of the manuscripts remain unstudied and uncatalogued, and their total number is unknown, affording only rough estimates." 700,000 and "a million" are not sourced here and are refused. Three attributed figures may be used with their attributions: HMML's 308,000 digitized manuscripts (4.35 million images), the Ahmed Baba Institute's holdings (given as "over 18,000" by Wikipedia and "nearly 30,000" by Gresham — quote the divergence or quote neither), and the 4,203 burned or stolen in 2012–13.
3. **Never date the surviving corpus to the medieval period.** The only fetched framing is "between the fifteenth and twentieth centuries." A large surviving corpus is evidence of a long scribal tradition, not of how many books sat in 16th-century Timbuktu. The claim that most surviving items are 18th-century-or-later copies is an unverified search-level lead recorded in Evidence Grade — do not repeat it either, in either direction.
4. **Refuse the 25,000-students figure.** It appears unhedged in Wikipedia's Timbuktu article and is contradicted by the same encyclopedia's dedicated article: 150–180 Qur'anic schools with 4,000–9,000 pupils learning basic literacy and recitation, and 200–300 advanced students from wealthy families. Deploy the granular numbers with their ranges, and note that most "students" were elementary pupils.
5. **Put the slaving in the claim, never in a footnote.** Mali and Songhai were slave-holding and slave-trading; Songhai "used slaves more consistently than their predecessors"; Timbuktu's wealth came from gold, salt, "and the trans-Saharan slave trade"; the most-quoted description of its market names slaves and Islamic books in the same clause. Trans-Saharan estimates run 6–10 million across thirteen centuries. Ahmad Baba's treatise argued against enslaving Muslims and against racial slavery, not against slavery — "he was not an advocate for ending the slave trade generally." A presentation that omits this is not a defence; it is an exhibit for the prosecution waiting to be opened.
6. **Do not launder the doctrinal gap into a defence.** The fetched record states the orthodox jurist position that no Muslim could be enslaved, and states in the same breath that "the majority of slaves were Muslims who should have been exempt from slavery because of their religious status." The gap between the law and the practice is the finding. Do not cite the rule as though it described the world, and do not cite the violation as though the rule never existed.
7. **Handle the chronicles with gloves.** The *Tarikh al-Sudan* is c. 1655, its author "rarely acknowledges his sources," its pre-1610 material is "presumably based on oral tradition," and he was chief secretary to the Moroccan Arma administration — a Timbuktu scholar writing under the conquerors. The *Tarikh al-Fattash* is worse: the Kati attribution is "an invention," Manuscript C is "a re-written forgery produced early in the 19th century by Nūḥ b. al-Ṭāhir," and the standard published text "is in fact a conflation of two separate works." Never quote "the Tarikh al-Fattash says" without that disclosure. This is a place where the honest version of the story is materially thinner than the popular one, and saying so is the card's credibility.
8. **Do not lean on Leo Africanus.** He is the most-quoted external witness to Timbuktu's book culture, and per the fetched record "it is even possible that he never crossed the Sahara," he may have "relied instead on information obtained from other travellers," and he wrote in Rome after capture and baptism under papal patronage. Cite him as a 16th-century European report whose author's presence is not secure, or leave him out.
9. **Keep Timbuktu inside its own boundaries.** Timbuktu is one Niger-bend city of roughly 50,000 in the 1500s. Djenné, Gao, and Walata have their own records; the 18th-century Mauritanian and 19th-century Sokoto traditions are separate developments this card does not anchor. "Timbuktu" is not a synonym for West Africa, and West Africa is not a synonym for Africa.
10. **Do not collapse the scholarly culture into a state achievement.** Mali and Songhai supplied patronage and security; Askia Muhammad "opened religious schools" and "recruited Muslim scholars from Egypt and Morocco." But the carriers were families and individual teachers, and the culture outlasted the polity — al-Sa'di wrote the *Tarikh al-Sudan* around 1655, sixty-four years after Songhai fell, from inside the conquerors' bureaucracy. Empire and scholarship are different objects and they did not die together.
11. **Do not make this a racial claim.** The lineage at this card's centre, the Aqit family, was Sanhaja Berber; Ahmad Baba's treatise argued specifically against *racial* slavery in a society where the enslaved were largely Black Africans. The defensible claim is geographic and civilizational — Islamic learning produced south of the Sahara — not a claim about any people's essence, in either direction.
12. **Do not import CMOS-0012's rejected overclaim.** Card 012 already refuses "the world's oldest university" for al-Qarawiyyin and refuses the madrasa-to-Europe influence thesis in both directions. Nothing here revives either. Timbuktu had *less* documented institutional machinery than the Nizamiyya, and this card makes no priority, influence, or transmission claim whatsoever.
13. **State the mechanism, not an essence.** What the sources show is a chain: trans-Saharan trade wealth (gold, salt — and human beings) financed a book trade; Arabic literacy arriving with Islam supplied a shared written language and a shared curriculum; royal patronage built and endowed mosques and recruited teachers; jurist families converted that into hereditary scholarly capital. That is a mechanism. "Islam produced this" is not — and the same trade that paid for the books trafficked the people.

## Safe Wording

> Between roughly the 14th and the late 16th centuries, Timbuktu on the Niger bend — under Mali and then Songhai patronage — sustained a substantial indigenous Islamic scholarly culture. It was not a university, and its own historians say so: there is no evidence of an organized educational institution there, and teaching ran through individual scholars with their own private students, in study circles seated on the floor, largely in scholars' houses, with three mosques (Djinguereber, built 1327; Sankore, 14th–15th century; Sidi Yahya, 1400) as focal points. What made it a culture rather than a scattering of tutors is documented: hereditary jurist families — the Sanhaja Berber Aqit lineage supplied the city's qadis and Sankore's imams, and one of them, al-Qadi al-Aqib (qadi 1565–1583), rebuilt or renovated four mosques between 1569 and 1579; an active book trade and private scholarly libraries; and locally written books, above all Ahmad Baba's forty-plus works and al-Sa'di's *Tarikh al-Sudan*, still the single most important source for the Songhai empire. The manuscripts survive in large numbers — one institution alone has digitized 308,000 of them — though their total is, by the sources' own statement, unknown, and the surviving corpus spans the fifteenth to the twentieth centuries rather than the medieval period alone. This coexisted with enslavement: Mali and Songhai were slave-holding, slave-trading states, Timbuktu's wealth came from gold, salt, and the trans-Saharan slave trade, and Ahmad Baba's treatise on the subject sought to stop Muslims enslaving Muslims rather than to end the trade. In 1591 a Moroccan army with firearms destroyed Songhai at Tondibi in about two hours; in 1593 many of Timbuktu's scholars were executed or exiled, and in 1594 Ahmad Baba and others were deported to Morocco with their manuscript collections. None of this makes Timbuktu a university, and none of it settles any comparison with Cairo, Fez, or Bologna. It shows something narrower and harder to dismiss: that Islamic learning in this period was written, taught, judged, and archived by people who lived south of the Sahara — produced there, not merely delivered there.

The Arabic scripts themselves "were developed in Timbuktu and the surrounding regions of Mali and West Africa," per the UCT project — the writing system was localized, not imported wholesale. And the architecture point cuts the same way: the story that an Andalusian architect designed Djinguereber has been reassessed, with the style now traced mainly to Saharan and West African building traditions. Language is not ownership. Latin scholastics wrote in Latin without being Romans; that a Sahelian jurist wrote in Arabic tells you which republic of letters he had joined, not who was doing the writing.
