## What this ledger is for

This file exists to prevent beautiful waqf stories from outrunning the evidence.

Every claim should distinguish:

1. **Founding certainty** — do we know when and by whom the endowment was founded?
2. **Legal-document certainty** — does a deed, register, or reliable edition survive?
3. **Service certainty** — what did the institution actually provide?
4. **Beneficiary certainty** — who could use it?
5. **Duration certainty** — for how long can operation be demonstrated?
6. **Continuity type** — same legal waqf, same asset, same building, same service, or only a successor institution?
7. **Motive complexity** — charity, piety, prestige, political control, dynastic strategy, asset protection, or mixed motives?
8. **Public wording safety** — what can be stated without brittle overclaiming?

### Duration labels

- **D1 — founding only:** foundation is documented; duration unknown.
- **D2 — multi-generation:** operation documented decades later.
- **D3 — multi-century:** operation/service documented at least 200 years later.
- **D4 — near-modern survival:** operation documented into the 19th/20th century.
- **D5 — living successor:** a descendant institution/service remains active today; legal continuity must be stated separately.

## WQF-001 — Haseki Hürrem Sultan Imaret, Jerusalem

**Status:** strong flagship; source hardening ongoing  
**Confidence:** High for founding, scale order-of-magnitude, and multi-century continuity; Medium for exact uninterrupted-service formulations  
**Duration label:** D5

### Claim

A Jerusalem public kitchen founded through Haseki Hürrem Sultan's waqf in 1552 fed hundreds of people daily and survives as a charitable institution on the original site nearly five centuries later, although its present legal conditions differ from the original Ottoman waqf.

### Strongest honest formulation

> **Hürrem Sultan endowed a Jerusalem charitable complex in 1552 whose public kitchen was designed to feed roughly 400–500 people twice daily; scholarship documents its operation over centuries, and a charitable institution remains active on the original site today under changed legal conditions.**

### What is well supported

- Founding year: 1552.
- Patron: Haseki Hürrem Sultan, wife of Sultan Suleiman I.
- Complex: public kitchen/imaret, mosque, lodging for pilgrims, and an inn/khan.
- Food distribution: Amy Singer's work discusses roughly 400–500 regular recipients and twice-daily service; the endowment prescribed substantial daily bread and soup production.
- Long duration: Ottoman-era account, repair, and administrative records document operation well beyond the founder's lifetime.
- Modern survival: Julien Loiseau describes the institution as still active at the same site today, while emphasizing changed legal conditions.

### Motive complexity

Do not reduce the institution to pure altruism. Ottoman imperial kitchens also projected legitimacy, integrated provincial economies, displayed dynastic generosity, and supported imperial policy.

That does not cancel the public benefit. It makes the institution historically real rather than saintly.

### Unsafe wording

- “The exact same waqf has operated unchanged since 1552.”
- “It fed 500 poor people twice a day every single day for almost 500 years.”
- “Hürrem invented the soup kitchen.”

### Public-safe summary

> **A sixteenth-century Ottoman endowment in Jerusalem was built to feed hundreds of people every day and became a charitable institution with extraordinary longevity.**

### Sources

- Amy Singer, *Constructing Ottoman Beneficence: An Imperial Soup Kitchen in Jerusalem* (SUNY Press, 2002). Princeton record: https://nes.princeton.edu/publications/constructing-ottoman-beneficence-imperial-soup-kitchen-jerusalem
- Amy Singer, “Serving Up Charity: The Ottoman Public Kitchen,” scholarly article; the widely cited ration figures should be checked against the published article/book before final quote deployment.
- Julien Loiseau, *La soif de Jerusalem*, ch. 4: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/791
- Abeer Qatanani, “The Charitable Foundation in the Ottoman Period: Al-Imara al-Amira in Islamicjerusalem and its impact on Social life”: https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/beytulmakdis/article/332466
- Serif Eroglu Memis, 2025 study of Haseki Sultan waqf management registers spanning 1687–1840: https://dergipark.org.tr/en/pub/iuturkiyat/article/1530417

### Next closure steps

- Obtain the 1552 deed edition/translation directly.
- Extract exact ration clauses.
- Build dated continuity checkpoints: 1552 / 1600s / 1700s / 1800s / 1900s / present.
- Identify current legal operator and services.

## WQF-002 — Sultan Suleiman's Hydraulic Waqf, Jerusalem

**Status:** strong flagship  
**Confidence:** High for foundation, intended public function, nine-fountain system, maintenance logic, and cross-community use evidence  
**Duration label:** D4 for Ottoman maintenance history; modern physical/legal status still to map

### Claim

Sultan Suleiman's 1541 hydraulic waqf converted Jerusalem's restored aqueduct and fountains into a funded public-maintenance system rather than a one-time construction project.

### Strongest honest formulation

> **In 1541, Sultan Suleiman endowed Jerusalem's restored water infrastructure so revenues would support its operation and repair; the system included public fountains serving the city, with contemporary evidence of use across religious communities and by travelers and animals.**

### Evidence

Julien Loiseau's archival study reproduces and translates the 1541 waqf documentation and traces later maintenance.

The system included nine public fountains. The source describes distribution across the Haram, urban neighborhoods, and a site outside the walls.

Loiseau notes that the outlying fountain served travelers, peasants, Bedouins, and animals. He also cites the 1537 Jewish visitor Uri Ben Simeon, who described Suleiman's new waterworks as sufficient for Jews, Muslims, and Christians.

The waqf's revenues functioned as an **intendance mechanism**: a way to fund ongoing maintenance and repair. Ottoman documentation records repeated intervention over centuries.

### Why this matters

This is a high-value bridge between Islamic political ethics and modern public-infrastructure questions.

The safest claim is not “Suleiman was a modern environmentalist.”

It is:

> **Water security was turned into a durable, endowed civic obligation.**

### Animal-mercy bridge

This is also a source-closed institutional example of public water infrastructure explicitly usable by animals. It should cross-link to the animal-mercy ledger, but without claiming the waqf was founded primarily as an animal-welfare project.

### Unsafe wording

- “Ottoman Jerusalem guaranteed equal water rights in a modern legal sense.”
- “The hydraulic waqf worked perfectly without interruption for 300 years.”
- “This proves Islamic rule was ecologically sustainable.”

### Sources

- Julien Loiseau, “Le waqf hydraulique de Soliman”: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/791
- Primary-document annex and translation: https://books.openedition.org/psorbonne/116571
- Sabrina Joseph, “Islamic law and the management of natural resources in seventeenth and eighteenth century Ottoman Syria,” *Environment and History* 21.2 (2015), DOI 10.3197/096734015x14267043141426; summary: https://zuscholars.zu.ac.ae/works/2176

### Next closure steps

- Map every fountain and surviving structure.
- Extract endowed revenue assets.
- Build maintenance/repair chronology.
- Determine current status of original waqf properties and waterworks.

## WQF-003 — Qalawun Bimaristan, Cairo

**Status:** major longevity case with wording caution  
**Confidence:** High that a 13th-century pious endowment remained in charitable use in 1844; Medium on uninterrupted hospital-function claims  
**Duration label:** D4

### Claim

The Maristan/Bimaristan of Sultan al-Mansur Qalawun was founded as a pious endowment in the thirteenth century and remained in charitable use more than five centuries later.

### Evidence checkpoint

Mine Ener's Cambridge article on nineteenth-century Egyptian poor relief cites an 1844 inspection of the Maristan Qalawun and explicitly identifies it as a hospital founded as a pious endowment in the thirteenth century. At that time men, women, and children were already living there as an “asylum for the poor.”

This gives a robust continuity marker from the late thirteenth century to 1844.

### What this does **not** yet prove

It does not, by itself, prove:

- uninterrupted operation as a hospital for ~560 years;
- unchanged services;
- unchanged financing;
- unchanged legal administration;
- modern hospital continuity.

### Public-safe summary

> **Qalawun's thirteenth-century endowed medical complex in Cairo was still functioning as a charitable refuge for poor people in 1844 — more than five centuries after its foundation.**

### Sources

- Mine Ener, “At the Crossroads of Empires: Policies toward the Poor in Early- to Mid-Nineteenth-Century Egypt,” *Social Science History*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-science-history/article/at-the-crossroads-of-empires/F3B8F3A443485BDC9145FCAC4290DB37
- Ahmed Ragab, *The Medieval Islamic Hospital: Medicine, Religion, and Charity*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/the-medieval-islamic-hospital/6038FE27C86A675A7CA62352EA51972E
- Metropolitan Museum of Art overview of Mamluk endowed public/pious foundations: https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-art-of-the-mamluk-period-1250-1517
- UNESCO Historic Cairo entry noting Qalawun Bimaristan among exceptional monuments of the city's scientific/medical history: https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/89/

### Next closure steps

- Obtain Qalawun waqf deed/translation.
- Establish exact opening date.
- Track documented medical function by century.
- Identify conversion to poorhouse/asylum and later status.

## WQF-004 — Rab'-i Rashidi Endowment, Tabriz

**Status:** design-sophistication case, not longevity case  
**Confidence:** High for deed and intended institutional design  
**Duration label:** D1/D2; do not use as proof of centuries-long survival

### Claim

The 1309 Rab'-i Rashidi waqf deed documents an unusually sophisticated endowed knowledge-and-welfare complex in Ilkhanid Tabriz.

### Evidence

UNESCO's Memory of the World entry describes a complex containing a paper mill, library, teaching hospital, orphanage, caravanserai, textile factory, teachers' training college, and seminary. The endowment sought, among other purposes, to ensure the copying and preservation of scholarly works.

The autograph waqf deed survives and is one of the oldest extant Iranian endowment charters.

### Why it matters

This is not primarily a continuity story. It is a **systems-design story**:

> knowledge preservation + production infrastructure + teaching + health + orphan care + lodging + manufacturing + permanent financing.

That is remarkably close to a vertically integrated knowledge institution.

### Sources

- UNESCO Memory of the World: https://www.unesco.org/en/memory-world/deed-endowment-rab-i-rashidi-rab-i-rashidi-endowment-13th-century-manuscript
- Hani Khafipour, “A Hospital in Ilkhanid Iran: Toward a Socio-economic Reconstruction of the Rab'-i Rashidi,” *Iranian Studies*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/iranian-studies/article/hospital-in-ilkhanid-iran-toward-a-socioeconomic-reconstruction-of-the-rabi-rashidi/FE33768C8D7C93E482F785BBCBFF2920

### Unsafe wording

- “Rab'-i Rashidi operated for centuries.”
- “It was the world's first university complex.”

## WQF-005 — Al-Azhar endowment portfolio persistence

**Status:** supporting evidence  
**Confidence:** High for 20th-century awqaf inventory snapshot  
**Duration label:** portfolio-level continuity, not one-wakf continuity

### Claim

Al-Azhar remained supported by multiple waqf endowments into the twentieth century, illustrating the ability of the endowment system to create a long-lived funding portfolio around an institution.

### Evidence

Daniel Crecelius reports that in 1963 Egypt's Ministry of Awqaf attempted to index endowments benefiting al-Azhar. One category listed 55 awqaf benefiting the mosque and another 80 benefiting Azhar institutes, and Crecelius believed even these lists were incomplete.

### Interpretation

This is strong evidence of **portfolio persistence**, but it should not be simplified into “the same waqf financed al-Azhar continuously for 1,000 years.”

### Source

- Daniel Crecelius, “The Organization of WAQF Documents in Cairo,” *International Journal of Middle East Studies*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-middle-east-studies/article/organization-of-waqf-documents-in-cairo1/316111797ABD667F022DCF3785CBD6C2

## WQF-006 — Waqf as decentralized public-goods institution

**Status:** high-level institutional claim  
**Confidence:** High that waqf was a major decentralized public-goods mechanism; disputed on long-run macroeconomic consequences

### Claim

Across the premodern Middle East, waqf was a major non-state mechanism for financing public goods and services.

### Supporting scholarship

Timur Kuran — despite being one of the institution's strongest critics — describes the waqf as a major long-run mechanism for decentralized public-goods provision.

His analysis is valuable precisely because it separates two propositions:

1. waqf provided extensive public goods;
2. waqf's classical legal structure also created long-run rigidity and governance problems.

Both can be true.

### Source

- Timur Kuran, “The Provision of Public Goods under Islamic Law: Origins, Impact, and Limitations of the Waqf System,” *Law & Society Review*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/law-and-society-review/article/abs/provision-of-public-goods-under-islamic-law-origins-impact-and-limitations-of-the-waqf-system/6653A7019DB49C8AE0542E017FDCEF54

## WQF-007 — Waqf failure mode: founder dead-hand and rigidity

**Status:** required counterevidence  
**Confidence:** High that this is a serious scholarly interpretation; macro-causal strength remains debated

### Claim

The same founder-lock that made waqf durable could also make it difficult to adapt to changing needs.

### Mechanism

- founder instructions could become too specific;
- managers sometimes had to circumvent original rules;
- obsolete purposes could trap assets;
- beneficiaries generally lacked modern governance voice;
- state confiscation could become easier as legitimacy eroded;
- waqf did not naturally evolve into a modern corporation with broad adaptive powers.

### Design lesson

> **Durability without adaptability becomes fossilization. Adaptability without mission lock becomes capture.**

Modern reimplementation needs both.

### Sources

- Kuran, *Law & Society Review* article above.
- Timur Kuran, “Why the Middle East is Economically Underdeveloped: Historical Mechanisms of Institutional Stagnation,” *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 18.3 (2004): https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/0895330042162421
- Timur Kuran, “Legal Roots of Authoritarian Rule in the Middle East,” *American Journal of Comparative Law*: https://academic.oup.com/ajcl/article-abstract/64/2/419/2682377

### Counter-research required

Do not turn Kuran's model into settled consensus. Search historians of Ottoman legal adaptation, cash waqf, administrative flexibility, local governance, and waqf reform for counterarguments and empirical nuance.

## WQF-008 — Non-Muslim use of waqf legal form

**Status:** important pluralism/institution-diffusion lead  
**Confidence:** High

### Claim

Christians and Jews living under Muslim rule also used the waqf legal form for their own endowments.

### Evidence

Ron Shaham's study of late Ottoman Palestine describes Christians and Jews using the Muslim waqf institution from the Middle Ages through the end of the Ottoman state, alongside their own religious endowment traditions.

### Why it matters

This does not prove waqf was uniquely Islamic in function; charitable endowment has analogues across traditions. It does show that the Islamic legal form could become useful enough to be adopted by religious minorities.

### Source

- Ron Shaham, “Christian and Jewish Waqf in Palestine during the late Ottoman period,” *Bulletin of SOAS*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/christian-and-jewish-waqf-in-palestine-during-the-late-ottoman-period1/61FCDFDE459AD0B25D3939596326081A

# Political-benevolence case queue

## PWR-001 — Umar II fiscal equalization

**Status:** promising; source stack incomplete  
**Confidence:** Medium-High for tax-treatment reform of converts; Low/Medium for later saintly anecdotes until audited

### Sourceable core

Modern scholarship drawing on al-Tabari and the fiscal-rescript tradition reports that Umar II ordered jizya lifted from converts and pushed against discriminatory treatment of non-Arab Muslims.

### Safe line

> **Umar II is historically important not merely because later Muslims remembered him as righteous, but because his short reign contains sourceable attempts to align taxation and convert status with a more universal Muslim equality.**

### Sources

- H.A.R. Gibb, “The Fiscal Rescript of Umar II,” *Arabica* 2.1 (1955).
- Oregon State open educational edition summarizing the rescript: https://open.oregonstate.education/ancientcivilizations/chapter/fiscal-rescript/
- “Islamization and Acculturation at the Time of Umar II (717–720),” *Al-Qantara*: https://al-qantara.revistas.csic.es/index.php/al-qantara/article/download/622/619

### Research caution

Later Muslim literature strongly idealized Umar II. Every restitution, personal-austerity, poverty-elimination, and “no one left to receive zakat” story needs separate provenance.

## PWR-002 — Nur al-Din and the Dar al-Adl

**Status:** major justice-institution lead  
**Confidence:** Medium-High for Damascus institution; lore details require source criticism

### Claim

Nur al-Din was associated with a `dar al-adl` in Damascus — a ruler-facing court of redress where the sovereign personally heard grievances.

### Evidence

Cambridge scholarship on Ayyubid petitions discusses the institution and cites the historical tradition that Nur al-Din held audience twice weekly. Another Cambridge study notes that Ibn al-Athir credited him with the first such Damascus institution while also warning that the earliest narrative source is pro-Zengid.

### Why it matters

The civilizational mechanism is more important than the hero story:

> **Justice was imagined not only as something subjects owed the state, but as something the ruler owed petitioners in a venue where executive authority could be invoked against abuse.**

### Sources

- D.S. Richards, “Petitions from the Ayyubid period,” *Bulletin of SOAS*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/438D26BABBEB2DE56BA9FC48A93D841E/S0041977X00100278a.pdf/petitions_from_the_ayyubid_period.pdf
- “The Ideological Significance of the Dar al-Adl in the Medieval Islamic Orient,” *International Journal of Middle East Studies*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-journal-of-middle-east-studies/article/ideological-significance-of-the-dar-aladl-in-the-medieval-islamic-orient/B68A4AFDF73C69B7746829AB34790C17

## PWR-003 — Saladin: beneficence and political instrument at once

**Status:** required anti-hagiography case  
**Confidence:** High that waqf was central to both public/religious patronage and political consolidation

### Claim

Saladin used awqaf to fund enduring institutions in Cairo and Jerusalem, but scholarship also shows that endowment policy served political and military consolidation and could involve properties taken from enemies.

### Why it matters

This is a model truth-constrained case:

> **The same institution can produce genuine public benefit and political power at the same time.**

That should be the default assumption until evidence shows otherwise.

### Source

- Yehoshua Frenkel, “Political and Social Aspects of Islamic Religious Endowments (awqaf): Saladin in Cairo (1169–73) and Jerusalem (1187–93),” *Bulletin of SOAS*: https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bulletin-of-the-school-of-oriental-and-african-studies/article/abs/political-and-social-aspects-of-islamic-religious-endowments-awqaf-saladin-in-cairo-116973-and-jerusalem-118793/A8E72C13BCE426F55D7736823B703C72

# Environmental/public-resource queue

## ENV-WAQF-001 — Waqf in natural-resource management

**Status:** strong research lane  
**Confidence:** High that waqf was integral to land/water management in Ottoman Syria

Sabrina Joseph's work on seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Ottoman Syria finds waqf integral to land and water-resource management and argues that Islamic legal practice often prioritized public good while adapting to local custom.

This gives a strong route into environmental history without projecting twenty-first-century ecological categories backward.

Source: https://zuscholars.zu.ac.ae/works/2176

**Status:** modern legal synthesis; historical implementation still to test

The 2026 *Cambridge Handbook of Islam and Environmental Law* identifies `hima`, `harim`, land-reclamation doctrine, and waqf among Islamic legal mechanisms relevant to ecological governance.

Source: https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-islam-and-environmental-law/environmental-stewardship-in-islamic-law/94B199CF113137FF915053AC827E95AB

# Canonical synthesis emerging from the evidence

The strongest present conclusion is institutional rather than triumphalist:

> **The waqf gave Islamic societies a repeatable way to convert private or dynastic assets into mission-bound revenue streams for public or pious purposes. In its best cases, this allowed a founder's obligation to outlive the founder by centuries. In its worst cases, the same mission lock could harden into rigidity, capture, or obsolescence.**

That is a pattern worth recovering because it contains both a civilizational achievement and a design warning.
