## Working complete rendering — NOT APPROVED FOR PUBLICATION AS A FINISHED TRANSLATION

> **1:1** In the name of God—the All-Merciful, the Ever-Merciful.  
> **1:2** All praise belongs to God, Lord of all worlds,  
> **1:3** the All-Merciful, the Ever-Merciful,  
> **1:4** Master of the Day of Reckoning.  
> **1:5** You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.  
> **1:6** Guide us along the straight path—  
> **1:7** the path of those whom You have favored, not of those upon whom anger has fallen, nor of those who have strayed.

This is **an editorial draft**. The 1:1 and 1:3 paired divine names and 1:7 wording are particularly provisional. Reader-friendly does not entitle us to bury ambiguity. Rhetorical punctuation is English editorial choice.

## Verse-by-verse Arabic and decisive translation choices

### 1:1 — invocation and mercy

**Arabic** (short display):  
بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

**Working English:** In the name of God—the All-Merciful, the Ever-Merciful.

- `bi-smi` = "in/with the name"; the grammatical phrase may imply an unexpressed action such as "I begin", **not** an explicit verb to insert into a close translation.
- `Allāh` is the Arabic divine name; using "God" in English is legitimate but not a perfect one-to-one reproduction of the name. Compare *Allah* as name versus "God" as standard English equivalent.
- `ar-Raḥmān` / `ar-Raḥīm` derive from ر ح م. They are **not just a single duplicated English adjective**, but assigning one name absolutely to universal mercy and the other exclusively to believers is a **commentarial interpretation**, not forced by morphology alone.
- `All-Merciful` and `Ever-Merciful` are ALSO interpretive. Keep alternative `the Universally Merciful, the Especially Merciful` visible as a teaching gloss, not a proof of exclusive English correctness. A final edition may preserve the divine names in transliteration with notes.
- **Decision pending:** human experts should assess whether retaining `ar-Raḥmān, ar-Raḥīm` better protects the distinct names than two English epithets.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/1) · [corpus morphology](https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=1&verse=1) · [Arabic grammar](https://corpus.quran.com/grammar.jsp?chapter=1&verse=1).

### 1:2 — praise and divine lordship

**Arabic:**  
ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

**Working English:** All praise belongs to God, Lord of all worlds,

- `al-ḥamd` spans praise and gratitude/thanksgiving; "praise" is compact, but a note should flag the wider connotation.
- `rabb` can convey Lord, Sustainer, Master, Nurturer, or one who fosters/maintains. "Lord" is traditional but can conceal the nurturing dimension.
- `al-ʿālamīn` = worlds/created orders/beings. "Of all worlds" is serviceable but can misleadingly conjure distinct planets. Avoid pretending a cosmology is resolved.
- `All` in "All praise" is a natural English force, not a separate adjective `kull` in the Arabic.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/2) · [corpus morphology](https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=1&verse=2).

### 1:3 — deliberate repetition, not English redundancy to delete

**Arabic:**  
ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ

**Working English:** the All-Merciful, the Ever-Merciful,

- Retain this repetition exactly in function. Repetition of divine names after lordship foregrounds mercy and cannot be omitted merely to streamline English style.
- Under the Kūfan verse scheme this is 1:3; some numbering schemes differ.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/3) · [corpus morphology](https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=1&verse=3).

### 1:4 — power, judgment, and an attested recitation variant

**Arabic — Ḥafṣ-style display:**  
مَـٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ

**Working English:** Master of the Day of Reckoning.

- `māliki` points toward owner/master. **`maliki` ("King") is also an established canonical qirāʾa**. That is not a spelling error; it is a genuine recitation difference with a meaning distinction. Do not falsely say the tradition has only one vocalization.
- `yawm al-dīn` can be represented "Day of Judgment", "Recompense", "Reckoning", or "Accountability"; *dīn* has more semantic range than "religion" alone.
- "Reckoning" is intended to preserve moral accountability without claiming that only punishment is involved. It is a translator's choice; alternative "Day of Recompense" better exposes the reward/punishment axis.
- **Potential intellectual mistake:** portraying mercy as the *only* attribute in this short surah ignores the deliberate adjacency of mercy to divine judgment.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/4) · [qirāʾāt presentation](https://quran.com/id/1%3A4/qiraat) · [Ibn Kathīr on the two readings in Arabic](https://www.altafsir.com/Tafasir.asp?LanguageId=1&UserProfile=0&tAyahNo=4&tDisplay=yes&tMadhNo=1&tSoraNo=1&tTafsirNo=7).

### 1:5 — exclusive worship and seeking help

**Arabic:**  
إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ

**Working English:** You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.

- `iyyāka` is fronted and **repeated**; this foregrounding is a meaningful exclusivity/emphasis cue. Retain both clauses rather than paraphrase into "we turn to you."
- `naʿbudu` often translated "worship" but the ʿ-b-d field also involves serving/devotion. "We serve" alone risks losing ritual worship.
- `nastaʿīn` = seek aid/help. **Do not infer** a prohibition on asking other humans for help from this English sentence alone; broader theology and context require separate arguments.
- The first-person **plural** is important: "we", not merely the individual's "I".
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/5) · [corpus syntax](https://corpus.quran.com/treebank.jsp?chapter=1&verse=5).

### 1:6 — a request for guidance, not a declaration of infallibility

**Arabic:**  
ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ

**Working English:** Guide us along the straight path—

- `ihdinā`: imperative/request addressed to God, object "us". It is a **prayer for guidance**, which itself carries a humility implication; that philosophical interpretation belongs here in notes, not stuffed into the verse.
- `ṣirāṭ` = path/way, `mustaqīm` = straight/upright. "Right way" may be more idiomatic but obscures the explicit path metaphor.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/6) · [corpus morphology](https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=1&verse=6).

### 1:7 — favored, anger, and straying

**Arabic:**  
صِرَٰطَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ غَيْرِ ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

**Working English:** the path of those whom You have favored, not of those upon whom anger has fallen, nor of those who have strayed.

- `anʿamta ʿalayhim` explicitly contains second-person "You" (the addressed God) granting favors. `favored` is defensible; `blessed` is an alternative.
- `al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim` uses a **passive participle**. Translating "those who earned Your anger" supplies agency/cause **and an identified agent** more directly than the morphology does. "Those upon whom anger has fallen" strives to keep the passive, but is less idiomatic; requires review.
- `aḍ-ḍāllīn` uses an **active participle** from the root associated with straying/going astray; do not erase that grammatical contrast.
- The verse **does not explicitly name Jews or Christians**. Some exegetical traditions associate these categories with particular groups, but importing those identifications into the primary English translation would collapse text and tafsīr.
- **Risk:** "not of ..." might be attached syntactically to the path/people in alternative readings; audit grammar rather than assuming the English structure is decisive.
- [Arabic verse](https://quran.com/1/7) · [corpus morphology](https://corpus.quran.com/wordbyword.jsp?chapter=1&verse=7) · [passive participle](https://corpus.quran.com/wordmorphology.jsp?location=%281%3A7%3A6%29).

## The surah's structure — a hypothesis to test, not injected into the verses

- **Invocation:** divine names focused on mercy (1:1).
- **Orientation:** praise, lordship, and mercy repeated (1:2–3).
- **Accountability:** the Day of Reckoning and divine sovereignty (1:4).
- **Relationship and agency:** worship and seeking help as a community (1:5).
- **Moral epistemology:** asking to be guided rather than self-certifying perfection (1:6–7).

This compressed arc may help readers: mercy and accountability are co-present; moral confidence is paired with a recurring request for guidance. **Do not describe this interpretive synthesis as an explicit verse-by-verse "algorithm" God specified.**

## Counterevidence and adverse review prompts

1. Does "All-Merciful/Ever-Merciful" smuggle duration and universality into epithets where morphology cannot specify them? Prefer retaining Arabic?
2. Are `rabb`, `ʿālamīn`, `dīn` and `ṣirāṭ` over-normalized into familiar English theological assumptions?
3. Are "Master" (Hafṣ) and "King" (other canonical recitations) both properly acknowledged, without privileging one as the only Qur'an?
4. Does "anger has fallen" become falsely impersonal when context points toward divine judgment? Does "earned wrath" over-attribute agency?
5. Does calling this a text-centered translation conceal non-Qur'an source dependencies in Arabic lexicography and manuscript studies? We must use such evidence openly.
6. Does the familiar English phrasing risk unintentional copying? Compare independent passages for originality and avoid large quotation of others' translations.
7. Have the selected passages been checked against a licensed, **version-locked** exact Arabic source and qirāʾa? **No — pending.**
8. Can an expert from a different exegetical tradition fairly reconstruct our decision from the editorial notes?
