## Working finding: law, not only sermons

Islamic jurists defined legally relevant obligations of animal care; hisba market-inspection jurisprudence addressed overloaded pack animals; Ottoman governments enacted animal-welfare regulations in identified legal codes **well before** Britain's landmark Cruel Treatment of Cattle Act (1822). This is not yet a proof of uniform enforcement, empire-wide compassion, or an earliest-in-humanity claim. It **is** enough to retire the false insinuation that the pre-1822 Islamic record is only scriptural advice.

### Claim AML-01 — 11th-century hisba enforcement and overburdened animals

- **Proposition:** Al-Māwardī (d. 1058) discussed overloading and failing to unload resting animals as wrongdoing within the responsibilities of the muḥtasib (market inspector) to correct; this demonstrates animal welfare in a legal-administrative theoretical system at least by the mid-eleventh century.
- **Evidence:** Necmettin Kızılkaya, "Be Gentle to Them: Animal Welfare and the Protection of Draft Animals in the Ottoman Fatwā Literature and Legislation," *Religions* 11:538 (2020), sec. 3, citing al-Māwardī n.d., p. 359 and al-Sakhāwī 1994, 68–70. https://doi.org/10.3390/rel11100538
- **Claim type:** juristic/administrative normative text; **not** independently verified surviving record of a specific 1050 market inspection.
- **Source-close gap:** obtain Arabic passage and specific edition of al-Māwardī, `al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya`, check translation and exact sanction; find parallel Ibn al-Ukhuwwa hisba passages.
- **Strong working line:** "By the eleventh century, Islamic market-inspection law addressed overloading animals." **Do not** equate al-Māwardī's year of death with an enacted law's effective date.

### Claim AML-02 — 13th-century rights of old, sick or no-longer-productive animals

- **Proposition:** ʿIzz al-Dīn ibn ʿAbd al-Salām (d. 1262), in `Qawāʿid al-aḥkām fī maṣāliḥ al-anām`, described animals' claims on humans, including maintenance even if aged or ill and not economically useful; prohibited burdens beyond their strength and other harms.
- **Primary Arabic witness**, section `حقوق البهائم والحيوان على الإنسان` on p. 167 of an online edition: https://islamweb.com/ar/library/content/39/199/
- **Independent scholarly context:** "God's Justice and Animal Welfare", https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2468553422000112 ; "Suffering the Sons of Eve: Animal Ethics in al-Maʿarrī’s Epistle of the Horse and the Mule" https://doi.org/10.3390/rel11080412.
- **Claim type:** juristic formulation of rights and duties. Is not, without additional evidence, a state pension system or universal actual retirement.
- **Source-close gap:** verify Arabic diacritics and book/edition page; distinguish normative `ḥuqūq` from retrospective legal terminology; investigate citations by later jurists and potential court practice.
- **Strong working line:** "A thirteenth-century Islamic jurist explicitly required care for animals even after illness or age made them unprofitable."

### Claim AML-03 — feeding and watering as obligations judicially enforceable in some schools

- **Proposition:** The modern `Kuwaiti Fiqh Encyclopedia` (vol. 18, entry `Ḥayawān`) compiles classical juristic positions: duty to feed/water animal even if yielding no benefit; majority legal interpretation allows judges to compel provision; Hanafi positions have a nuanced split about judicial compulsion.
- **Compilation source:** https://www.fiqhpedia.org/articles/article:kwfe:v18:a044?lang=en (section `Spending on Animals and Being Kind to Them`, cited reference to `Rawḍat al-Ṭālibīn`, `al-Mughnī`, `Kashshāf al-Qināʿ` and other classical texts).
- **Claim type:** **later reference digest of classical legal opinions**, not itself dated medieval court records.
- **Source-close gap:** inspect the named passages in the underlying legal manuals; date individual editions and periods; do not overstate consensus on **judicial** compulsion where schools disagree.
- **Working line:** "Classical Islamic jurists regarded feeding working animals as an owner's obligation; most juristic positions also allowed legal compulsion when owners neglected them."

### Claim AML-04 — 1502–1507 enacted Ottoman municipal animal-protection rules

- **Proposition:** Ottoman `Iḥtisāb` regulations issued between **1502 and 1507** under Bāyezīd II included **Bursa Article 74** (horseshoes required before riding/loading) and **Istanbul Article 58** (care for animals' feet, correct saddles, no overburdening, avoidance of running injured-footed horses).
- **Evidence:** Kızılkaya 2020 §3, citing `Kanunnâme-i İhtisâb-i Bursa` (1935 edition), Akgündüz 1990 (pp. 209, 295): https://doi.org/10.3390/rel11100538
- **Type:** formal Ottoman regulations, not merely Qur'anic instruction or a private fatwa. Exact city-year per code is not fixed by this source; use 1502–1507 for the group.
- **Working line:** "Istanbul's early-sixteenth-century market regulations already prohibited overburdening horses, mules and donkeys and required proper animal care."
- **Source-close gap:** obtain editions/archival identifiers for both texts, verify original Ottoman Turkish/Arabic wording, jurisdiction and implementation.

### Claim AML-05 — punishable Ottoman violations, 16th-century edicts and court jurisdiction

- **Proposition:** Selim I-era legal code (1512–1520) provided punishment for violations involving load-carrying animals, including loss of entitlement to use the affected animal according to Article 156; **19 March 1587** imperial order to Istanbul's qadi prohibited loads beyond capacity on horses/mules and prescribed punishment for noncompliance.
- **Evidence:** Kızılkaya 2020 §3, citing Akgündüz 1990 pp. 110, Akgündüz 1991 p. 115, Refik 1336 pp. 95–96. https://doi.org/10.3390/rel11100538
- **Type:** enacted government regulations and documented legal penalties. **Do not** confuse textually prescribed punishment with evidence of how frequently courts imposed it.
- **Working line:** "Ottoman legal codes didn't just recommend humane treatment: they threatened court punishment for overloading working animals."
- **Source-close gap:** retrieve actual qadi archival case files of enforcement, determine penalty and representative frequency.

### Claim AML-06 — British comparison grounded to statute's nature and scope

- **Proposition:** Britain enacted the `Cruel Treatment of Cattle Act` / `Martin's Act` in **1822** (3 Geo. 4 c. 70), criminalizing certain cruelties against defined farm and draft animals. This was a **landmark nineteenth-century British parliamentary statute**, not the origin of animal ethics or animal protection anywhere in the world.
- **Evidence:** UK House of Commons parliamentary materials and American Bar Association historical review: https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200405/cmselect/cmenvfru/52/52ii.pdf ; https://dev.americanbar.org/groups/international_law/resources/international-law-news/2022-summer/two-hundred-years-enactment-worlds-first-animal-protection-law/ ; original statute references 3 Geo. 4 c.70.
- **Comparison interval:** 1822 minus a source date somewhere within 1502–1507 gives **315–320 years**. Avoid "exactly 320 years" if code's year not locked.
- **Strong working line:** "Ottoman officials regulated the mistreatment of working animals roughly three centuries before Britain's 1822 parliamentary anti-cruelty act."
- **Comparative boundary:** statutes have distinct jurisdictions, legal traditions, coverage, punishment and enforcement; the evidence still permits a meaningful **law-to-law** chronological comparison.

## Hierarchy of possible headlines — don't demote grounded law to opinion

1. **Working, strongest with current sources:** "OTTOMAN LAWS PROTECTED WORKING ANIMALS 300+ YEARS BEFORE BRITAIN'S 1822 LANDMARK ACT." Specify type and date in small footer.
2. **Older tradition, supported as historical juristic theory:** "BY THE 11TH CENTURY, ISLAMIC LAW ADDRESSED ANIMAL OVERLOAD IN MARKET INSPECTION." Do not say a dated court conviction occurred without proof.
3. **Moral surprise, 13th-century original passage:** "A MEDIEVAL ISLAMIC JURIST SAID TO CARE FOR AN ANIMAL EVEN WHEN IT COULD NO LONGER WORK." Source exact text and page.
4. **The mechanism:** "AN ANIMAL'S RIGHT TO CARE COULD OUTLAST ITS USEFULNESS." Label as statement from specific jurists rather than universally enforced right across all Muslim states.
5. **Owner legal duty:** "THE OWNER COULD BE COMPELLED TO FEED THE ANIMAL." Use "many jurists held" not every jurisdiction always did.

### Avoid weaker and stronger errors

- **False minimization:** "Islamic world had animal teachings, but Britain had laws." **Remove.**
- **False superlative:** "Islam invented animal protection law before everyone." Earlier Indian and Jewish traditions, other contemporaneous law and customs complicate that assertion.
- **False enforcement:** "All Ottoman animals had protection enforced by courts." The norms are documented; typical outcomes still require archives.
- **False firsts:** "Britain's 1822 law was humanity's first animal-cruelty law." The often repeated phrase in modern legal histories needs jurisdictionally scoped contextualization.
- **Terminology:** `fiqh` is Islamic jurisprudence/legal doctrine; `ḥisba` entails administrative oversight; `kanunnāme` is a legal code; a fatwa is a juristic opinion; a historical court judgment is evidence of implementation. Never compress all into "just religion," and never treat all as identical legal instruments either.

## Proposed synthesis

**The evidence currently permits an unembarrassed historical correction:** Islamic animal welfare reached formal law and state regulation long before Britain's famous 1822 legislation; it is a research error to represent the former as only ethical teaching and the latter as the first legal step.

**What remains open:** extent and consistency of real-life enforcement, how Ottoman law compares to particular contemporaries, and whether religious inspiration or other incentives chiefly produced legal outcomes.

**Source certainty:** juristic passages (strongly attested in named publications), official-code provisions (strong peer-reviewed editions-based report), judiciary records (research needed for measured implementation).
