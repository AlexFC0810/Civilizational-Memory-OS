# Move One Community — Migration Playbook

## Universal CTA

> **Do not merely leave a platform. Bring one community with you.**

The atomic unit of migration is the community, not the isolated individual.

## Why Individual Exit Fails

People remain on captured platforms because their relationships, audiences, institutions, and routines are there. Moving alone often produces an empty room and confirms the belief that alternatives are not viable.

Community migration changes the equation:

- people retain social context;
- norms and trust move together;
- administrators can support onboarding;
- usage begins immediately;
- problems become observable;
- and the destination gains a durable node rather than a dormant account.

## Participation Ladder

### Level 1 — Map

Document current dependencies:

- identity;
- member list or audience;
- archives;
- discovery;
- moderation;
- payments;
- events;
- documents;
- private communication;
- integrations;
- and recovery procedures.

Output: a dependency and risk map.

### Level 2 — Claim

Establish a portable or open identity and reserve community names where appropriate.

Output: recoverable identities and verified ownership records.

### Level 3 — Mirror

Create an open-system presence while keeping the existing platform active.

Mirror suitable public content, announcements, archives, or discussion spaces. Avoid fake migration claims when the community remains inactive at the destination.

Output: a functioning bridge and onboarding test.

### Level 4 — Move

Bring one complete social unit:

- group chat;
- creator community;
- team;
- congregation;
- civic organization;
- professional network;
- online forum;
- or local institution.

Output: a live community with an explicit operating home.

### Level 5 — Maintain

Create durable capacity for:

- hosting;
- moderation;
- onboarding;
- documentation;
- backups;
- funding;
- governance;
- and incident response.

Output: a community that can survive beyond the initial champion.

### Level 6 — Build

Turn migration failures and limitations into documented infrastructure gaps, specifications, bounties, integrations, or public-interest projects.

Output: ecosystem improvement rather than private workaround alone.

## Pilot Intake Questions

1. What kind of community is moving?
2. How many active and total members exist?
3. What functions are mission-critical?
4. What must remain public, private, searchable, or archived?
5. What safety and moderation risks exist?
6. What legal, regulatory, or confidentiality constraints apply?
7. What platforms and integrations are currently used?
8. What data can be exported?
9. What would cause members to refuse the move?
10. What technical and financial capacity is available?
11. Is full migration necessary, or is mirroring sufficient initially?
12. What would count as success after 30 and 90 days?

## Migration Plan Template

### A. Community Profile

- name;
- purpose;
- size;
- leadership and administrators;
- member demographics relevant to accessibility;
- current platforms;
- critical workflows.

### B. Sovereignty Risks

- vendor lock-in;
- identity loss;
- inaccessible exports;
- moderation dependence;
- payment dependence;
- archive loss;
- single-administrator dependence;
- hosting or app-store choke points.

### C. Target Stack

For every selected tool, record:

- function;
- governance;
- hosting;
- identity model;
- portability;
- moderation;
- cost;
- recovery;
- known limitations;
- fallback.

### D. Migration Sequence

1. Export and back up.
2. Establish identities and administrator access.
3. Configure destination and safety controls.
4. Recruit a small test cohort.
5. Run onboarding and recovery tests.
6. Mirror content or communication.
7. Publish the member-facing migration case.
8. Move the first complete workflow.
9. Measure usage and repair failure points.
10. Decide whether to expand, pause, or roll back.

### E. Member Communication

Explain in ordinary language:

- why the move is happening;
- what members gain;
- what they must do;
- what remains unchanged;
- what may be inconvenient;
- how to get help;
- how privacy and moderation work;
- whether the old platform remains available.

Never sell decentralization through jargon alone.

### F. Support Roles

Assign:

- migration lead;
- technical administrator;
- community communicator;
- moderation and safety lead;
- archive and data steward;
- onboarding helpers;
- measurement owner;
- escalation contact.

### G. Rollback and Continuity

Define:

- export checkpoints;
- backup locations;
- communication fallbacks;
- administrator recovery;
- vendor-failure response;
- criteria for pausing or reversing the move.

## Three Founding Pilot Types

### 1. Creator Community

Tests public identity, audience portability, discovery, content distribution, monetization, and cross-posting.

### 2. Civic or Religious Community

Tests private communication, governance, calendars, documents, moderation, institutional memory, and continuity.

### 3. Technically Sophisticated Online Community

Tests hosting, interoperability, protocol bridges, open development, and contributor workflows.

## Pilot Case File

Every pilot should publish a truth-first case study containing:

- starting conditions;
- feared losses;
- selected stack and rationale;
- time and money required;
- member adoption;
- failures and friction;
- safety incidents;
- retention at 30 and 90 days;
- unresolved dependencies;
- infrastructure gaps created;
- recommendation for similar communities.

Failures are not embarrassment. They are the roadmap.

## Success Metrics

- percentage of active members onboarded;
- weekly active use;
- retention after 30 and 90 days;
- workflows successfully transferred;
- help requests per member;
- administrator hours required;
- cost per active member;
- number of fatal dependencies removed;
- member-reported trust and usability;
- ability to export and recover;
- contribution back to the open ecosystem.

## Minimum Viable Sovereignty Completion Check

The migration is not complete until the community can:

- export its data;
- preserve its relationships;
- change providers;
- communicate outside one vendor;
- retain an independent archive;
- understand governance;
- survive the failure or capture of one provider.

## Public Message

> **Do not disappear into an empty alternative. Move with the people who make the space alive.**
