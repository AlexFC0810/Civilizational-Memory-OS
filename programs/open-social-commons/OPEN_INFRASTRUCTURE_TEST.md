# The Open Infrastructure Test

**Purpose:** A public evaluation constitution for digital systems that claim to be open, decentralized, user-owned, federated, community-governed, or public-interest infrastructure.

The test exists to prevent the Commons from becoming a promotional directory or sponsor-driven ranking system.

## Core Principle

> **Open code is not enough. A system is meaningfully open only when power remains visible, contestable, portable, and difficult to recapture.**

## Evaluation Dimensions

Score each dimension from 0 to 5 and attach evidence, tradeoffs, and unresolved questions. Do not collapse the result into one misleading number without preserving the underlying profile.

### 1. Exit

- Can users export their data in usable formats?
- Can they preserve identity, audience, relationships, subscriptions, and history?
- Can a community migrate without rebuilding from zero?
- Are exports complete, timely, documented, and interoperable?

### 2. Identity Portability

- Is identity tied to one company or application?
- Can users move hosts or clients while retaining recognition and relationships?
- How does account recovery work?
- Can a provider seize or permanently strand the identity?

### 3. Social-Graph Portability

- Can follows, memberships, subscriptions, contacts, and community ties move?
- Is the social graph readable and usable by compatible systems?
- Does migration preserve consent and privacy?

### 4. Hosting and Infrastructure Pluralism

- Can multiple independent parties host the system?
- How difficult and expensive is hosting?
- Is one relay, cloud, index, app store, or gateway a practical choke point?
- Can communities self-host without becoming full-time infrastructure companies?

### 5. Client Choice

- Can independent developers build compatible applications?
- Can users change clients without changing identity or community?
- Does one dominant client retain hidden platform-like power?

### 6. Discovery and Algorithm Choice

- Can users choose chronological, community, or third-party feeds?
- Can independent ranking and search providers exist?
- Are recommendation criteria inspectable or explainable?
- Can the owner privilege itself or favored actors without detection?

### 7. Moderation Choice and Safety

- Can communities choose or combine moderation systems?
- Are block lists, labels, filters, and appeals portable where appropriate?
- Can vulnerable users protect themselves without granting one institution unlimited control?
- How are fraud, harassment, exploitation, illegal content, and coordinated abuse handled?

### 8. Governance

- Who can change protocol rules, product rules, and community rules?
- How are maintainers selected and removed?
- Can users, hosts, workers, and affected communities exercise meaningful voice?
- Are decisions, rationales, conflicts, and votes documented?

### 9. Forkability and Continuity

- Can another team continue the code or protocol after capture, abandonment, or failure?
- Are trademarks, domains, keys, data schemas, and critical infrastructure independently survivable?
- Is the system legally and technically forkable or only theoretically open source?

### 10. Funding Transparency

- Who funds the project?
- How concentrated is donor or investor power?
- Are major contracts, grants, sponsorships, and conflicts visible?
- Can one patron withdraw support or dictate policy?

### 11. Economic Sustainability

- Can the system survive without surveillance advertising, speculative tokens, or permanent billionaire subsidy?
- Who pays, who benefits, and who bears the hidden costs?
- Does the business model reward captivity, outrage, extraction, or genuine service?

### 12. Ownership and Capture Resistance

- Can the project be sold?
- Can investors, founders, governments, or donors acquire decisive control?
- Are mission locks, foundations, cooperatives, steward ownership, or other protections real and enforceable?
- What happens after commercial success?

### 13. Privacy and Surveillance

- What data is collected, inferred, retained, shared, sold, or exposed?
- Can users participate with minimal data disclosure?
- Are metadata and social relationships protected?
- Does decentralization increase uncontrolled replication of sensitive data?

### 14. Security and Recovery

- How are keys, credentials, compromised accounts, and lost devices handled?
- Can ordinary users recover safely?
- Are irreversible mistakes presented as ideological virtue?
- Is critical infrastructure independently audited?

### 15. Accessibility and Usability

- Can a normal person join, understand, and use the system?
- Is onboarding available across devices, languages, abilities, and technical levels?
- Are safety, recovery, and migration comprehensible?
- Does openness impose hidden labor on users or volunteer administrators?

### 16. Cultural and Community Pluralism

- Can communities establish different norms without becoming isolated islands?
- Does one prestige hierarchy, celebrity layer, or dominant culture reconcentrate attention?
- Can minority communities govern themselves while remaining interoperable?

### 17. Public Verifiability

- Are code, governance, funding, incidents, audits, and major decisions inspectable?
- Are claims independently reproducible?
- Are limitations and failures disclosed as clearly as strengths?

## Minimum Viable Digital Sovereignty Threshold

A system should not be presented as a sufficient community migration destination unless the community can reasonably:

1. export its data;
2. preserve member relationships;
3. change providers;
4. communicate outside one vendor;
5. retain an independent archive;
6. understand governance;
7. survive the failure or capture of one provider.

## Required Output Format

Every project profile should include:

- project name and category;
- problem addressed;
- maturity and current use;
- governance and ownership;
- protocol and license;
- hosting model;
- identity model;
- portability details;
- discovery and algorithm model;
- moderation and safety model;
- funding and business model;
- strengths;
- limitations;
- concentration risks;
- migration difficulty;
- best-fit users and communities;
- evidence links;
- unresolved questions;
- date of last review.

## Integrity Rules

- Sponsorship cannot purchase a higher rating.
- Reviewers must disclose conflicts.
- Projects can submit corrections and evidence but cannot veto criticism.
- Scores must be revised when evidence changes.
- Strong claims require strong evidence.
- Technical decentralization must not conceal economic, cultural, discovery, or governance centralization.
- No project receives an “open” seal solely because its source code is public.

## Final Question

> **If this system succeeds, what prevents it from becoming the next privately controlled gatekeeper?**
