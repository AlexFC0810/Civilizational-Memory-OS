# Open Social Commons — 90-Day Launch Plan

## Objective

End the first 90 days with:

- a credible public doctrine;
- an evaluation system;
- a working solutions map;
- a migration intake process;
- a contributor and funder routing layer;
- and three real pilot communities underway.

The objective is not merely to launch a website.

> **The first proof is a community that can survive the failure or capture of one vendor.**

## North-Star Measures

By Day 90, target:

- 20–30 serious projects mapped;
- 10 projects fully reviewed through the Open Infrastructure Test;
- 3 pilot communities selected;
- at least 1 complete workflow moved or mirrored in each pilot;
- public gap records generated from pilot friction;
- 25+ qualified contributors or institutional contacts mapped;
- at least 3 fundable public-good opportunities defined;
- baseline 30-day retention and support-burden data collected where timing permits.

## Phase 1 — Foundation and Signal Collection

### Days 1–15

#### Establish the public kernel

Publish:

- the public manifesto;
- the Open Infrastructure Test;
- the Move One Community CTA;
- the minimum viable digital sovereignty standard;
- the public conflict-of-interest and funding rules.

#### Create the landscape schema

Define structured records for:

- protocols;
- social applications;
- communications systems;
- identity systems;
- moderation tools;
- collective-intelligence tools;
- governance systems;
- funding infrastructure;
- hosting tools;
- ownership models.

#### Begin stakeholder map

Identify:

- protocol and application builders;
- civic-technology organizations;
- digital-rights groups;
- cooperative and steward-ownership experts;
- creators with captive-audience concerns;
- religious and civic institutions;
- public-interest funders;
- researchers;
- migration-tool builders;
- accessibility and safety experts.

#### Launch listening process

Collect answers to:

- What prevents your community from moving?
- What do you fear losing?
- Which alternative have you tried?
- Where did onboarding fail?
- What missing tool would change your decision?
- What would make you trust an evaluator?

Output: founding concern and obstacle map.

## Phase 2 — Atlas, Evaluation, and Pilot Recruitment

### Days 16–35

#### Publish the first Solutions Atlas cohort

Create 20–30 structured project profiles.

Do not attempt equal depth immediately. Separate:

- mapped;
- reviewed;
- pilot-tested;
- and recommended-for-specific-use.

#### Complete first full evaluations

Apply the Open Infrastructure Test to at least 10 projects.

Each evaluation must preserve:

- strengths;
- limitations;
- concentration risks;
- best-fit use cases;
- unresolved questions;
- evidence and date of review.

#### Recruit pilot communities

Select three different pilots:

1. creator community;
2. civic or religious community;
3. technically sophisticated online community.

Selection criteria:

- real need;
- identifiable community champion;
- willingness to document failures;
- manageable scope;
- sufficient member consent;
- meaningful but not catastrophic risk;
- ability to measure results.

#### Form advisory circle

Recruit a small, politically heterogeneous group covering:

- technical architecture;
- community operations;
- moderation and safety;
- governance;
- funding;
- law and policy;
- accessibility;
- creator economics.

The advisory circle does not own the Commons. It increases error correction.

## Phase 3 — Pilot Design and Movement Infrastructure

### Days 36–60

#### Complete dependency maps

For each pilot, document:

- identity;
- social graph;
- data and archives;
- moderation;
- private communication;
- public communication;
- payments;
- events;
- documents;
- integrations;
- recovery;
- fatal dependencies.

#### Select target stacks

Use the Open Infrastructure Test and community needs. Do not force one protocol onto every pilot.

#### Build migration plans

For each pilot:

- export and backup plan;
- administrator and recovery setup;
- small test cohort;
- member communication;
- onboarding support;
- moderation and safety configuration;
- workflow sequence;
- rollback criteria;
- success metrics.

#### Launch coordination board

Create visible opportunities organized by:

- contribution needed;
- estimated effort;
- project owner;
- status;
- evidence of need;
- public-good value;
- pilot connection.

#### Launch transparent funding mechanism

At minimum disclose:

- money received;
- source and concentration;
- intended use;
- spending;
- milestones;
- responsible party;
- remaining balance.

## Phase 4 — Execute Pilots and Publish the Truth

### Days 61–90

#### Move or mirror one complete workflow per pilot

Examples:

- creator announcements and subscriber conversation;
- congregation or civic-group communications and documents;
- technical-community development and governance workflow.

#### Measure friction

Track:

- setup time;
- administrator hours;
- support requests;
- member conversion;
- active usage;
- missing features;
- data loss;
- safety incidents;
- recovery failures;
- cost.

#### Convert failure into gap records

Every material obstacle becomes a Commons Gap Map entry with:

- affected users;
- existing partial solutions;
- missing component;
- public-interest specification;
- contributors needed;
- pilot community;
- completion condition;
- funding target.

#### Publish pilot field reports

State clearly:

- what worked;
- what failed;
- what was harder than expected;
- what remains platform-dependent;
- what similar communities should do;
- what builders and funders can solve next.

Do not manufacture success stories.

## Founding Coalition Map

Build relationships across distinct entry points:

### Builders

Open-social protocols, clients, identity, hosting, moderation, search, migration, and interoperability.

### Community Operators

Creators, congregations, civic organizations, local institutions, online communities, unions, cooperatives, educators, and journalists.

### Evaluators

Researchers, security reviewers, digital-rights experts, accessibility specialists, governance scholars, and user advocates.

### Capital

Public-interest foundations, aligned donors, cooperative finance, fiscal hosts, bounties, and transparent crowdfunding.

### Narrative and Culture

Creators, designers, writers, documentarians, educators, and trusted messengers across political constituencies.

## Governance Before Scale

Before significant funding or visibility, establish:

- public conflict-of-interest rules;
- sponsor noninterference;
- review correction process;
- decision log;
- data and repository export process;
- founder continuity and succession plan;
- anti-harassment and contributor conduct rules;
- procedure for challenging evaluations;
- prohibition on pay-to-rank.

## Kill Criteria and Course Correction

Pause or redesign if:

- the site becomes a static directory without migration activity;
- one protocol faction captures the agenda;
- sponsor interests distort evaluations;
- pilots cannot identify real community champions;
- the project accumulates attention while producing no infrastructure movement;
- technical purity overrides normal-user needs;
- founder dependency increases rather than decreases;
- safety and recovery are treated as secondary.

## Day-90 Review Questions

1. Did any community remove a fatal digital dependency?
2. Which project evaluations changed a real decision?
3. Where did normal users fail?
4. Which missing component would unlock the most migration?
5. What did pilots reveal that doctrine missed?
6. Is the Commons becoming more forkable and less founder-dependent?
7. Did attention convert into contributors, money, pilots, or infrastructure?
8. What should be stopped, continued, or doubled?

## Closing Standard

> **Do not judge the first 90 days by how impressive the website looks. Judge them by whether the Commons helped real people preserve identity, relationships, memory, and collective agency outside one private gatekeeper.**
